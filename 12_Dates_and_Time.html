1How MySQL stores dates and times

Think of a wall calendar and a clock. A date is a page on the calendar: 19 November 2024. A time is a reading on the clock: 10:30 in the morning. Many things need both, like a train ticket, which says which day and what time. MySQL has a type for each:

TypeHoldsLooks likeIn our data
DATEA day2024-11-19clean_customers.signup_date
DATETIMEA day and a time2024-11-19 10:30:00orders.order_date, web_events.event_time
TIMESTAMPA day and a time, adjusted for time zones2024-11-19 10:30:00Not used. Section 11 shows the difference.
TIMEA time of day, or a length of time10:30:00Not used

Dates are always written from the biggest part to the smallest: year, month, day, then hours, minutes, seconds. That's why MySQL wants '2024-11-19' and not '19/11/2024', and it's why Day 11 had to convert the other formats.

The orders in online_store_full have a time as well as a date. DATE() drops the clock and keeps the day, and TIME() does the opposite. You'll use DATE() constantly for "per day" questions:

SQL
SELECT order_id,
       order_date,
       DATE(order_date) AS just_the_day,
       TIME(order_date) AS just_the_time
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_id = 5012;
Result
+----------+---------------------+--------------+---------------+
| order_id | order_date          | just_the_day | just_the_time |
+----------+---------------------+--------------+---------------+
|     5012 | 2024-10-08 13:34:15 | 2024-10-08   | 13:34:15      |
+----------+---------------------+--------------+---------------+

(That's the order from the "future" that Day 11 moved back to 2024.)

What is "today"?

CURDATE() gives today's date, and NOW() gives the date and time right now, from the database server's clock:

SQL
SELECT CURDATE(), NOW();   -- the answer changes every time you run it

That's exactly what you want in a live dashboard: "orders in the last 7 days" should always mean the 7 days before now. But it's a problem when you need to reproduce a number. A report that says "142 customers are inactive" will say something else next week. When a number goes into a slide or an email, write the date it was measured on, and use that fixed date in the SQL. That's what we'll do today with 31 December 2024.

2Taking a date apart

A date is made of parts, and MySQL has a function to pull out each one. Here they are on order 5012:

SQL
SELECT order_date,
       YEAR(order_date)      AS yr,
       QUARTER(order_date)   AS qtr,
       MONTH(order_date)     AS month_num,
       MONTHNAME(order_date) AS month_name,
       DAY(order_date)       AS day_num
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_id = 5012;
Result
+---------------------+------+------+-----------+------------+---------+
| order_date          | yr   | qtr  | month_num | month_name | day_num |
+---------------------+------+------+-----------+------------+---------+
| 2024-10-08 13:34:15 | 2024 |    4 |        10 | October    |       8 |
+---------------------+------+------+-----------+------------+---------+
SQL
SELECT order_date,
       DAYNAME(order_date)   AS day_name,
       DAYOFWEEK(order_date) AS day_of_week,
       WEEKDAY(order_date)   AS weekday_num,
       HOUR(order_date)      AS hr,
       DAYOFYEAR(order_date) AS day_of_year
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_id = 5012;
Result
+---------------------+----------+-------------+-------------+------+-------------+
| order_date          | day_name | day_of_week | weekday_num | hr   | day_of_year |
+---------------------+----------+-------------+-------------+------+-------------+
| 2024-10-08 13:34:15 | Tuesday  |           3 |           1 |   13 |         282 |
+---------------------+----------+-------------+-------------+------+-------------+

Most of these are what you'd guess. QUARTER splits the year into four: January to March is 1, October to December is 4. DAYOFYEAR counts from 1 January, so 8 October is day 282.

Two ways to number the days of the week. 8 October 2024 was a Tuesday. DAYOFWEEK says 3, because it counts Sunday as 1. WEEKDAY says 1, because it counts Monday as 0. Some countries start the week on Sunday and others on Monday, and MySQL has a function for each. Mixing them up shifts every day by one, so check which one you're using. Today we'll use WEEKDAY, with weeks starting on Monday.

3Filtering by date the right way

On Day 5 you used BETWEEN for dates, with a warning: it only works safely on DATE columns. Our order_date is a DATETIME. Here's the trap, for real. How many orders were placed in December 2024?

SQL
SELECT 'BETWEEN 1 Dec AND 31 Dec' AS filter_used, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_date BETWEEN '2024-12-01' AND '2024-12-31'
UNION ALL
SELECT '>= 1 Dec AND < 1 Jan', COUNT(*)
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_date >= '2024-12-01' AND order_date < '2025-01-01';
Result
+--------------------------+--------+
| filter_used              | orders |
+--------------------------+--------+
| BETWEEN 1 Dec AND 31 Dec |    374 |
| >= 1 Dec AND < 1 Jan     |    384 |
+--------------------------+--------+

Ten orders disappeared. When you compare a DATETIME with '2024-12-31', MySQL reads it as '2024-12-31 00:00:00': midnight at the very start of the 31st. So BETWEEN stops there, and every order placed later that day is left out:

SQL
SELECT order_id, order_date, status
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_date >= '2024-12-31' AND order_date < '2025-01-01'
ORDER BY order_date
LIMIT 3;
Result
+----------+---------------------+---------+
| order_id | order_date          | status  |
+----------+---------------------+---------+
|     6135 | 2024-12-31 03:39:18 | Pending |
|     6136 | 2024-12-31 08:50:14 | Pending |
|     6137 | 2024-12-31 10:30:47 | Pending |
+----------+---------------------+---------+

The fix is the pattern from Day 5: greater than or equal to the first day, and strictly less than the day after the last day. It's called a half-open range, because it includes the start and excludes the end. It works for every period, whatever the column type, and you never have to remember whether a month has 30 or 31 days: December is >= '2024-12-01' AND < '2025-01-01', and February is >= '2024-02-01' AND < '2024-03-01', leap year or not.

What about YEAR(order_date) = 2024 AND MONTH(order_date) = 12? It also gives 384, and it's easy to read. The catch is speed. Wrapping the column in a function means MySQL has to calculate YEAR() and MONTH() for every row in the table before it can compare, and it can't use an index on order_date to jump straight to December. On our 6,000 orders you won't notice. On 60 million, you will. Day 18 explains indexes. For now, build the habit: leave the column alone, and put the range on the other side.

4Date arithmetic

Moving forward and back: INTERVAL

To move a date, add or subtract an INTERVAL: a number and a unit (DAY, WEEK, MONTH, QUARTER, YEAR, HOUR, MINUTE...). Day 11 used one to fix the future orders.

SQL
SELECT '2024-03-15' + INTERVAL 10 DAY            AS plus_10_days,
       '2024-03-15' - INTERVAL 2 WEEK            AS minus_2_weeks,
       '2024-03-15' + INTERVAL 3 MONTH           AS plus_3_months,
       DATE_ADD('2024-03-15', INTERVAL 1 YEAR)   AS plus_1_year;
Result
+--------------+---------------+---------------+-------------+
| plus_10_days | minus_2_weeks | plus_3_months | plus_1_year |
+--------------+---------------+---------------+-------------+
| 2024-03-25   | 2024-03-01    | 2024-06-15    | 2025-03-15  |
+--------------+---------------+---------------+-------------+

DATE_ADD(date, INTERVAL ...) and DATE_SUB do the same as + and -. Use whichever reads better to you.

Months are the tricky unit, because they have different lengths. What is "31 January plus one month"? There's no 31 February, so MySQL gives you the last day of February instead:

SQL
SELECT '2024-01-31' + INTERVAL 1 MONTH                     AS jan31_2024_plus_1,
       '2023-01-31' + INTERVAL 1 MONTH                     AS jan31_2023_plus_1,
       '2024-01-31' + INTERVAL 1 MONTH + INTERVAL 1 MONTH  AS plus_1_then_1,
       '2024-01-31' + INTERVAL 2 MONTH                     AS plus_2,
       LAST_DAY('2024-02-10')                              AS last_day_of_feb;
Result
+-------------------+-------------------+---------------+------------+-----------------+
| jan31_2024_plus_1 | jan31_2023_plus_1 | plus_1_then_1 | plus_2     | last_day_of_feb |
+-------------------+-------------------+---------------+------------+-----------------+
| 2024-02-29        | 2023-02-28        | 2024-03-29    | 2024-03-31 | 2024-02-29      |
+-------------------+-------------------+---------------+------------+-----------------+

Look at the middle two. Adding one month twice lands on 29 March, but adding two months at once lands on 31 March. Once a date has been pushed back to the 29th, it stays there. If you need month ends, don't step from one to the next. Use LAST_DAY(date), which always gives the last day of that date's month, leap years included.

The gap between two dates: DATEDIFF and TIMESTAMPDIFF

You met DATEDIFF(later, earlier) on Day 7. Its bigger cousin, TIMESTAMPDIFF(unit, earlier, later), can count in any unit. Careful: the order of the two dates is reversed between them. They also count differently, which matters when times are involved. Take an order at 11pm and a payment at 1am the next morning:

SQL
SELECT DATEDIFF('2024-03-02 01:00:00', '2024-03-01 23:00:00')             AS datediff_days,
       TIMESTAMPDIFF(DAY,  '2024-03-01 23:00:00', '2024-03-02 01:00:00')   AS full_days,
       TIMESTAMPDIFF(HOUR, '2024-03-01 23:00:00', '2024-03-02 01:00:00')   AS full_hours,
       TIMESTAMPDIFF(MONTH, '2024-01-31', '2024-02-29')                    AS months_a,
       TIMESTAMPDIFF(MONTH, '2024-01-15', '2024-03-14')                    AS months_b;
Result
+---------------+-----------+------------+----------+----------+
| datediff_days | full_days | full_hours | months_a | months_b |
+---------------+-----------+------------+----------+----------+
|             1 |         0 |          2 |        0 |        1 |
+---------------+-----------+------------+----------+----------+
  • DATEDIFF ignores the times and counts calendar days crossed: 1 March to 2 March is 1.
  • TIMESTAMPDIFF counts complete units. Only 2 hours passed, so that's 0 complete days.
  • The same goes for months: MySQL counts a month as complete only when the day of the month reaches the starting day again. February has no 31st, so 31 January to 29 February is 0 complete months. And 15 January to 14 March is only 1.

Neither is wrong. They answer different questions. "Did the payment come the next day?" is a DATEDIFF question. "Did it come within 24 hours?" is TIMESTAMPDIFF(HOUR, ...) < 24.

A real use: how long since each customer bought?

The number of days since a customer's last order is called recency. It's one of the most useful numbers in e-commerce, because a customer who bought last week is far more likely to buy again than one who bought last year:

SQL
WITH last_order AS (
    SELECT customer_id, MAX(order_date) AS last_order_date
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT CASE
           WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-31', last_order_date) <= 30  THEN '1. 0-30 days'
           WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-31', last_order_date) <= 90  THEN '2. 31-90 days'
           WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-31', last_order_date) <= 180 THEN '3. 91-180 days'
           WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-31', last_order_date) <= 365 THEN '4. 181-365 days'
           ELSE '5. Over a year'
       END AS days_since_last_order,
       COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM last_order
GROUP BY days_since_last_order
ORDER BY days_since_last_order;
Result
+-----------------------+-----------+
| days_since_last_order | customers |
+-----------------------+-----------+
| 1. 0-30 days          |       248 |
| 2. 31-90 days         |       382 |
| 3. 91-180 days        |       290 |
| 4. 181-365 days       |       451 |
| 5. Over a year        |       744 |
+-----------------------+-----------+

Notice '2024-12-31' where you'd normally write CURDATE(): that's the fixed "today" from section 1. Of the 2,115 customers who have ever completed an order, 744 (about a third) haven't bought anything for over a year. That's a list the marketing team would like to see. (The numbers in the labels, 1. to 5., are there so ORDER BY puts the bands in the right order. Text sorts letter by letter, so without them "181-365 days" would come before "31-90 days", because "1" comes before "3".) On Day 13, recency becomes the R in RFM.

5Grouping by day, week, month and quarter

On Day 6 you grouped by month with DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m'). The idea works for any period: turn each date into a period key, a value that's the same for every date in that period, and group by it.

PeriodKey8 Oct 2024 becomes
DayDATE(order_date)2024-10-08
Week (starting Monday)DATE(order_date) - INTERVAL WEEKDAY(order_date) DAY2024-10-07
MonthDATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m-01')2024-10-01
QuarterCONCAT(YEAR(order_date), '-Q', QUARTER(order_date))2024-Q4

The week key needs a moment. WEEKDAY says how many days we are past Monday (Tuesday is 1), so subtracting that many days always lands on the Monday that started the week. For the month, '%Y-%m-01' gives the first day of the month. It groups exactly like Day 6's '%Y-%m', but it looks like a real date, so you can compare it with dates and join it to the calendar you'll build in section 8.

Why not WEEK()? MySQL has a WEEK(date) function that gives a week number. It has eight different modes, and the numbers don't mean what most people expect around New Year:

SQL
SELECT d,
       DAYNAME(d)      AS day_name,
       WEEK(d)         AS week_default,
       WEEK(d, 3)      AS iso_week,
       YEARWEEK(d, 3)  AS iso_year_and_week
FROM (SELECT DATE('2023-01-01') AS d
      UNION ALL SELECT '2024-01-01'
      UNION ALL SELECT '2024-12-30') AS sample_days;
Result
+------------+----------+--------------+----------+-------------------+
| d          | day_name | week_default | iso_week | iso_year_and_week |
+------------+----------+--------------+----------+-------------------+
| 2023-01-01 | Sunday   |            1 |       52 |            202252 |
| 2024-01-01 | Monday   |            0 |        1 |            202401 |
| 2024-12-30 | Monday   |           52 |        1 |            202501 |
+------------+----------+--------------+----------+-------------------+

1 January 2024 is week 0 by default. In the ISO standard (mode 3), 1 January 2023 belongs to week 52 of 2022, and 30 December 2024 belongs to week 1 of 2025. Anyone grouping by "week 1" without the year gets a mix of two years. Label weeks by their start date, as above, and the question disappears.

7When do people shop?

Taking dates apart answers some of the most practical questions a business has: which days to run promotions, when to send emails, when the warehouse needs more staff. First, by day of the week:

SQL
SELECT DAYNAME(order_date)             AS day_name,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)        AS orders,
       ROUND(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) * 100
             / SUM(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_orders,
       ROUND(SUM(line_total) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)) AS avg_order_value
FROM clean_sales
GROUP BY day_name, WEEKDAY(order_date)
ORDER BY WEEKDAY(order_date);
Result
+-----------+--------+---------------+-----------------+
| day_name  | orders | pct_of_orders | avg_order_value |
+-----------+--------+---------------+-----------------+
| Monday    |    736 |          13.0 |            2302 |
| Tuesday   |    744 |          13.1 |            2231 |
| Wednesday |    753 |          13.3 |            2292 |
| Thursday  |    787 |          13.9 |            2336 |
| Friday    |    800 |          14.1 |            2435 |
| Saturday  |    943 |          16.7 |            2385 |
| Sunday    |    899 |          15.9 |            2289 |
+-----------+--------+---------------+-----------------+

Sorting by day_name would give alphabetical order (Friday, Monday, Saturday...). Grouping by WEEKDAY(order_date) as well lets us sort Monday to Sunday. Saturday and Sunday are the busiest days, with about 16% of orders each, against 13 to 14% on weekdays. The SUM(...) OVER () is Day 9's trick for a percentage of the grand total.

Now by time of day. Twenty-four hours is a lot of rows, so group the hours into parts of the day with CASE:

SQL
SELECT CASE
           WHEN HOUR(order_date) < 6  THEN '1. Night (12am-6am)'
           WHEN HOUR(order_date) < 12 THEN '2. Morning (6am-12pm)'
           WHEN HOUR(order_date) < 17 THEN '3. Afternoon (12pm-5pm)'
           WHEN HOUR(order_date) < 22 THEN '4. Evening (5pm-10pm)'
           ELSE '5. Late night (10pm-12am)'
       END AS part_of_day,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
       ROUND(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) * 100
             / SUM(COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_orders
FROM clean_sales
GROUP BY part_of_day
ORDER BY part_of_day;
Result
+---------------------------+--------+---------------+
| part_of_day               | orders | pct_of_orders |
+---------------------------+--------+---------------+
| 1. Night (12am-6am)       |    258 |           4.6 |
| 2. Morning (6am-12pm)     |   1235 |          21.8 |
| 3. Afternoon (12pm-5pm)   |   1465 |          25.9 |
| 4. Evening (5pm-10pm)     |   2029 |          35.8 |
| 5. Late night (10pm-12am) |    675 |          11.9 |
+---------------------------+--------+---------------+

More than a third of all orders come in the evening, between 5pm and 10pm: after work and after dinner. An email that lands at 7pm has a much better chance than one at 3am. (Notice the boundaries: each WHEN uses <, the half-open idea again, so 5:59am is night and 6:00am is morning, with no gaps or overlaps.)

8Filling the gaps: a calendar table

On Day 9 you used LAG to compare each month with the one before, with a warning: LAG looks at the previous row, not the previous month. If a month has no sales, it has no row, and LAG quietly compares with the wrong month. Here's that happening, on the Winter Jacket (product 37) in 2024:

SQL
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m') AS order_month,
           SUM(quantity)                    AS units
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE product_id = 37
      AND order_date >= '2024-01-01'
    GROUP BY order_month
)
SELECT order_month,
       units,
       LAG(order_month) OVER (ORDER BY order_month) AS compared_with,
       LAG(units)       OVER (ORDER BY order_month) AS previous_units
FROM monthly
ORDER BY order_month;
Result
+-------------+-------+---------------+----------------+
| order_month | units | compared_with | previous_units |
+-------------+-------+---------------+----------------+
| 2024-01     |    12 | NULL          |           NULL |
| 2024-02     |     1 | 2024-01       |             12 |
| 2024-03     |     2 | 2024-02       |              1 |
| 2024-04     |     2 | 2024-03       |              2 |
| 2024-08     |     1 | 2024-04       |              2 |
| 2024-09     |     1 | 2024-08       |              1 |
| 2024-10     |     3 | 2024-09       |              1 |
| 2024-11     |    26 | 2024-10       |              3 |
| 2024-12     |    17 | 2024-11       |             26 |
+-------------+-------+---------------+----------------+

Nobody buys a winter jacket in May, June or July, so those months simply aren't there, and August gets compared with April. A chart of this would draw a straight line from April to August, as if sales had held steady all summer. The fix is to start from a list of all the months, and attach the sales to it. Months with nothing to attach get 0.

Build a calendar

Think of a printed wall calendar: it has a square for every day, including the days when nothing happened. A calendar table is the same thing in your database: one row per day, plus handy columns like the month, the week and the day name. Almost every company's data warehouse has one (Day 17 calls it a "date dimension").

How do you make 1,461 rows without typing them? With a recursive CTE: a CTE that refers to itself. It starts with one row (1 January 2022), and then keeps adding "the previous day plus one" until it reaches the last day. Day 16 explains exactly how it works. For now, treat it as a recipe. First, a safety limit you'll run into:

SQL
WITH RECURSIVE days AS (
    SELECT DATE('2022-01-01') AS d
    UNION ALL
    SELECT d + INTERVAL 1 DAY FROM days WHERE d < '2025-12-31'
)
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM days;
MySQL says
ERROR 3636 (HY000): Recursive query aborted after 1001 iterations. Try increasing @@cte_max_recursion_depth to a larger value.

MySQL stops a recursive CTE after 1,000 rounds, in case a mistake makes it run forever. Four years of days needs more, so we raise the limit for this session first. Here's the calendar:

Build 1: the calendar table
SET SESSION cte_max_recursion_depth = 2000;

DROP TABLE IF EXISTS calendar;

CREATE TABLE calendar (
    cal_date    DATE        PRIMARY KEY,
    cal_year    SMALLINT    NOT NULL,
    cal_quarter TINYINT     NOT NULL,
    cal_month   TINYINT     NOT NULL,
    month_start DATE        NOT NULL,   -- first day of the month
    month_label CHAR(8)     NOT NULL,   -- e.g. 'Nov 2024', for display only
    week_start  DATE        NOT NULL,   -- the Monday that starts the week
    day_name    VARCHAR(9)  NOT NULL,
    is_weekend  TINYINT     NOT NULL    -- 1 for Saturday and Sunday
);

INSERT INTO calendar
WITH RECURSIVE days AS (
    SELECT DATE('2022-01-01') AS d
    UNION ALL
    SELECT d + INTERVAL 1 DAY FROM days WHERE d < '2025-12-31'
)
SELECT d,
       YEAR(d),
       QUARTER(d),
       MONTH(d),
       d - INTERVAL (DAY(d) - 1) DAY,
       DATE_FORMAT(d, '%b %Y'),
       d - INTERVAL WEEKDAY(d) DAY,
       DAYNAME(d),
       WEEKDAY(d) >= 5
FROM days;

Everything in the SELECT is from this article. d - INTERVAL (DAY(d) - 1) DAY goes back to the 1st of the month (from the 19th, go back 18 days), and gives a real DATE rather than text. WEEKDAY(d) >= 5 is true (1) for Saturday and Sunday. The calendar covers 2022 to 2025, a bit wider than our data, so there's room on both sides. Let's look at the end of February 2024:

Use it: start from the calendar, then LEFT JOIN

SQL
WITH jacket AS (
    SELECT DATE(order_date) AS sale_date, quantity
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE product_id = 37
),
monthly AS (
    SELECT c.month_start,
           COALESCE(SUM(j.quantity), 0) AS units
    FROM calendar c
    LEFT JOIN jacket j ON j.sale_date = c.cal_date
    WHERE c.cal_date >= '2024-01-01' AND c.cal_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY c.month_start
)
SELECT month_start,
       units,
       LAG(units) OVER (ORDER BY month_start) AS previous_units
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month_start;

9Comparing periods: last month, last year

A number on its own means little. "₹7 lakh in December" is only good or bad compared with something. The two classic comparisons are month over month (MoM: versus the month before) and year over year (YoY: versus the same month last year). With a gap-free monthly series, both are a LAG:

SQL
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT c.month_start,
           COALESCE(SUM(s.line_total), 0) AS revenue
    FROM calendar c
    LEFT JOIN clean_sales s ON DATE(s.order_date) = c.cal_date
    WHERE c.cal_date >= '2023-01-01' AND c.cal_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY c.month_start
),
compared AS (
    SELECT month_start,
           revenue,
           LAG(revenue)     OVER (ORDER BY month_start) AS last_month,
           LAG(revenue, 12) OVER (ORDER BY month_start) AS same_month_last_year
    FROM monthly
)
SELECT DATE_FORMAT(month_start, '%b %Y') AS month,
       ROUND(revenue) AS revenue,
       ROUND((revenue - last_month) * 100 / last_month, 1)                     AS vs_last_month_pct,
       ROUND((revenue - same_month_last_year) * 100 / same_month_last_year, 1) AS vs_last_year_pct
FROM compared
WHERE month_start >= '2024-01-01'
ORDER BY month_start;

LAG(revenue, 12) means "12 rows back". That's only the same month last year if every month has a row, which is why we built the series from the calendar. The year filter is in the outer query, after the window functions have run, so the 2024 rows can still see 2023 (Day 9's rule: WHERE runs before window functions).

Read the two columns together:

  • Month over month jumps around with the seasons. October is up 57.5% (the festive sales), and February is down 22.9%. That's the shop's normal yearly rhythm, not good or bad news.
  • Year over year takes the season out, because it compares October with October. Every month of 2024 beat the same month of 2023. That's why YoY is the number most businesses watch.
  • And then there's December: down 29.2% on November, and only 10.9% up on last December, the weakest growth of the year. Is something wrong?

The incomplete period trap

Look at what had happened to the orders placed in the last days of each December by the time our data was exported:

Comparing it with a finished December is unfair.

The fix is a like-for-like comparison: compare the same days in both years, using only days that have had time to settle. Up to 24 December:

       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(line_total))   AS revenue_1_to_24_dec
FROM clean_sales
WHERE (order_date >= '2023-12-01' AND order_date < '2023-12-25')
   OR (order_date >= '2024-12-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-25')
GROUP BY yr;

This trap catches people every month in real jobs: the current month is always unfinished, and the last few days always look weak. Before reporting any period that ends near "today", ask whether it has had time to settle.

Year to date

"Year to date" (YTD) means everything from 1 January up to now. It's a running total that starts again each year, which is Day 9's running total with PARTITION BY year:

SQL
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT c.month_start,
           COALESCE(SUM(s.line_total), 0) AS revenue
    FROM calendar c
    LEFT JOIN clean_sales s ON DATE(s.order_date) = c.cal_date
    WHERE c.cal_date >= '2023-01-01' AND c.cal_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY c.month_start
),
running AS (
    SELECT month_start,
           SUM(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY YEAR(month_start) ORDER BY month_start) AS ytd
    FROM monthly
),
compared AS (
    SELECT month_start, ytd,
           LAG(ytd, 12) OVER (ORDER BY month_start) AS ytd_last_year
    FROM running
)
SELECT DATE_FORMAT(month_start, '%b')               AS up_to_end_of,
       ROUND(ytd)                                   AS ytd_2024,
       ROUND(ytd_last_year)                         AS ytd_2023,
       ROUND((ytd - ytd_last_year) * 100 / ytd_last_year, 1) AS growth_pct
FROM compared
WHERE month_start >= '2024-01-01'
ORDER BY month_start;

10Rolling averages: seeing the trend through the noise

Daily numbers jump around: a big order here, a quiet Tuesday there. A rolling average (also called a moving average) smooths them out. The 7-day rolling average for a day is the average of that day and the 6 before it. Each day, the window moves forward by one. It's like judging your mood by the last week rather than by this morning.

SQL
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT c.cal_date,
           COALESCE(SUM(s.line_total), 0) AS revenue
    FROM calendar c
    LEFT JOIN clean_sales s ON DATE(s.order_date) = c.cal_date
    WHERE c.cal_date >= '2024-12-01' AND c.cal_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY c.cal_date
),
smoothed AS (
    SELECT cal_date,
           revenue,
           AVG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY cal_date
                              ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS avg_7_days
    FROM daily
)
SELECT cal_date,
       DATE_FORMAT(cal_date, '%a') AS day_name,
       ROUND(revenue)              AS revenue,
       ROUND(avg_7_days)           AS avg_7_days
FROM smoothed
WHERE cal_date >= '2024-12-15'
ORDER BY cal_date;
Result

| 2024-12-17 | Tue      |   14733 |      23410 |

Daily revenue swings between about ₹8,000 and ₹49,000. The 7-day average moves much more calmly, between about ₹23,000 and ₹29,000, until Christmas. Two details:

  • The window counts rows, so every day must have a row. ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW means "this row and the 6 rows before it". That's 7 days only because the calendar gives every day a row, including 29 to 31 December with 0. Without the calendar, a missing day would quietly stretch the window to 8 days.
  • The days are calculated from 1 December, but shown from the 15th. That way, the 15th's average can use the 9th to the 14th. Filter after the window has done its work (Day 9).

And the end of the table shows the incomplete period trap again, this time as a chart would show it: the average sinks to ₹6,706 by 31 December. It's not a crash. It's orders that haven't completed yet, plus three days of zeros. Real dashboards often grey out the most recent days for exactly this reason.

Another way: RANGE with an INTERVAL. MySQL can also define the window by dates rather than rows: AVG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY sale_date RANGE BETWEEN INTERVAL 6 DAY PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW). That looks back 6 calendar days, even when some days are missing. But it only averages the days that have rows, so a day with no sales is left out instead of counted as 0, and the average comes out too high. With a calendar, you get the honest version.

11Time zones, and a checklist of date traps

The shop records every time in India time (IST), which is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of UTC, the world's reference clock. That matters as soon as the data meets another country. An order placed at 1am on 1 January in Mumbai happened at 7:30pm on 31 December in London. "Which year was it?" depends on the time zone.

CONVERT_TZ(datetime, from_zone, to_zone) converts between zones:

SQL
SELECT order_id,
       order_date                                         AS india_time,
       CONVERT_TZ(order_date, '+05:30', '+00:00')         AS time_in_utc,
       CONVERT_TZ(order_date, 'Asia/Kolkata', 'UTC')      AS using_zone_names
FROM clean_orders
WHERE order_id = 5012;
Result
+----------+---------------------+---------------------+------------------+
| order_id | india_time          | time_in_utc         | using_zone_names |
+----------+---------------------+---------------------+------------------+
|     5012 | 2024-10-08 13:34:15 | 2024-10-08 08:04:15 | NULL             |
+----------+---------------------+---------------------+------------------+

The version with offsets ('+05:30') always works. The version with names ('Asia/Kolkata') returned NULL, because a fresh MySQL install like ours doesn't have its time zone tables loaded. A database admin can load them once, and then names work too. Names are better for countries that change their clocks in summer, like the UK and the USA, because a fixed offset can't know about daylight saving.

DATETIME or TIMESTAMP?

Both store a date and a time, but they behave differently when time zones are involved. A DATETIME stores exactly what you give it, like writing on paper. A TIMESTAMP converts the value to UTC when it's saved, and back to your session's time zone when it's read. Watch:

SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE tz_demo (
    saved_as_datetime  DATETIME,
    saved_as_timestamp TIMESTAMP
);

SET time_zone = '+05:30';   -- pretend we're in India
INSERT INTO tz_demo VALUES ('2024-11-19 10:00:00', '2024-11-19 10:00:00');

SET time_zone = '+00:00';   -- now read it from London
SELECT * FROM tz_demo;

SET time_zone = DEFAULT;    -- back to the server's setting
Result
+---------------------+---------------------+
| saved_as_datetime   | saved_as_timestamp  |
+---------------------+---------------------+
| 2024-11-19 10:00:00 | 2024-11-19 04:30:00 |
+---------------------+---------------------+

Same value saved twice. Read from a different time zone, the DATETIME still says 10:00, but the TIMESTAMP now says 4:30, which is the same moment on a London clock. (A TEMPORARY table disappears when you disconnect, which makes it handy for experiments like this. The last line puts your session's time zone back, so NOW() and any TIMESTAMP columns behave normally again.) TIMESTAMP is great for "when did this happen" in systems used across countries. Its limits: it only covers 1970 to January 2038, and the same row can look different to different users, which surprises people. Many teams store everything as DATETIME in UTC, and convert only for display.

🎯 Interview corner

Date questions are among the most common in SQL interviews: month-over-month growth, rolling averages and missing dates come up again and again.

Concept questions

Q1What's the difference between DATE, DATETIME and TIMESTAMP?

DATE stores a day only. DATETIME stores a day and a time exactly as given. TIMESTAMP also stores a day and a time, but MySQL converts it to UTC when saving and back to the session's time zone when reading, so the same row can show different times to users in different zones. TIMESTAMP only covers 1970 to January 2038.

Q2Why can BETWEEN give the wrong answer for dates?

On a DATETIME column, BETWEEN '2024-12-01' AND '2024-12-31' stops at 2024-12-31 00:00:00, so everything later on the 31st is missed. The safe pattern is a half-open range: col >= '2024-12-01' AND col < '2025-01-01'. It works for every column type and every month length.

Q3Why is WHERE YEAR(order_date) = 2024 considered bad practice?

It returns the right rows, but wrapping the column in a function means the database has to calculate it for every row and can't use an index on order_date. Writing order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND order_date < '2025-01-01' gives the same rows and lets an index do the work. Conditions that can use an index are called sargable.

Q4What's the difference between DATEDIFF and TIMESTAMPDIFF?

DATEDIFF(later, earlier) returns days, ignoring the time part, so 11pm to 1am the next day is 1. TIMESTAMPDIFF(unit, earlier, later) counts complete units of any size (seconds to years), so the same pair is 0 days or 2 hours. Note the argument order is reversed between them.

Q5How do you show months (or days) that have no sales?

Start from a complete list of periods, usually a calendar table (or one generated with a recursive CTE), and LEFT JOIN the sales to it, with COALESCE(SUM(...), 0). For several dimensions, such as category by month, CROSS JOIN the lists first to make the full grid. Filters on the sales side must go in the ON clause or a CTE, not the WHERE, or the empty rows disappear again.

Q6How do you calculate year-over-year growth for each month?

Build a monthly series with no gaps, then use LAG(revenue, 12) OVER (ORDER BY month) to bring last year's value onto the same row: growth = (this year - last year) / last year × 100. The series must have no missing months, or 12 rows back won't be 12 months back. Alternatively, join the monthly table to itself on month = previous month + INTERVAL 1 YEAR.

Q7Why might the latest month look much worse than earlier months?

Because it's often incomplete: either the month isn't over, or recent orders haven't reached their final status (completed, delivered, paid) yet. Compare like-for-like (the same days in the previous period), wait until the period has settled, or mark recent periods as provisional.

Q8What is a calendar (date dimension) table, and why use one?

A table with one row per day and useful columns such as year, quarter, month start, week start, day name, weekend flag, and often holidays or financial-year periods. It fills gaps in time series, makes grouping by any period a simple join, and keeps definitions like "our week starts on Monday" in one place.

           order_date,

  ON second_o.customer_id  = first_o.customer_id
 AND first_o.order_number  = 1

Quick recap

  • DATE is a day, DATETIME a day and a time, and TIMESTAMP a day and a time that follows the session's time zone. DATE() and TIME() split a DATETIME.
  • Take dates apart with YEAR, QUARTER, MONTH, DAY, HOUR, DAYNAME. Watch out: DAYOFWEEK starts at Sunday = 1, WEEKDAY at Monday = 0.
  • Filter with half-open ranges: >= the start and < the day after the end. BETWEEN on a DATETIME loses the last day. Keep the column bare.
  • Move dates with + INTERVAL n UNIT. Measure gaps with DATEDIFF (calendar days) or TIMESTAMPDIFF (complete units). Use LAST_DAY for month ends.
  • Group by a period key: DATE(), the week's Monday, '%Y-%m-01', or year and quarter. Avoid WEEK() numbers. Format with DATE_FORMAT only at the end, and never sort by the label.
  • A calendar table plus LEFT JOIN fills the days and months with no data, so LAG, YoY and rolling averages compare the right periods. Sometimes the gap is the finding.
  • Compare with LAG(revenue) for month over month and LAG(revenue, 12) for year over year. YoY removes seasonality. YTD is a running total partitioned by year.
  • The latest period is usually unfinished. Compare like-for-like: December 1 to 24 grew about 28%, not 11%.
