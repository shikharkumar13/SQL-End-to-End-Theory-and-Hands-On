Day 21's review ended with a question it couldn't answer. November's returning customers weren't drifting away, "at least not yet", but whether customers stay over months, not days, was left for today. Day 13 made a promise too: monthly cohorts, and a proper measure of churn. Today's case study does both, for a manager with a decision to make. As on Day 21, there are no big new SQL features, only three small functions (MOD, SQRT and CRC32), each explained where it appears. The skill is in the definitions: what "lost" means, which customers to leave out, and how to tell a real difference from luck.

Before you start. Run USE online_store_full;. You'll use the Day 11 clean layer, Day 12's calendar, Day 13's customer_rfm view and Day 21's festive_sale_days view. Today adds two views of its own, in the Build boxes.

1The brief

Farah Siddiqui, the Head of Marketing (employee 3, reporting to Ritu), sends this:

"I have budget for one win-back campaign in January. Before I spend it: how many customers are we losing? Are we getting better or worse at keeping them? What makes someone come back? And who should I contact first?"

Four questions, and they have to be answered in a sensible order: you can't say who to win back until you've decided what "lost" means. So the sections follow the questions, with one step added at the start, the one Day 21 taught: check the data first.

A shop like ours has a problem a phone company doesn't. When someone cancels a phone contract, there's a date and a letter. When someone stops buying from a shop, nothing happens. Think of a regular at a tea stall: one day they just stop coming, and the owner only notices weeks later that they haven't been around. So churn in a shop has to be defined, from silence. Section 4 does that with data, not guesswork.

Two settings for the whole day:

  • "Today" is the end of 24 December 2024, the last settled day (Day 12, and Day 20's trap 2). Orders from the last few days of December are left out, so nobody looks more active, or more silent, than they really are.
  • A customer is a master customer ID with completed orders, exactly as in clean_sales (Day 11). Someone with two accounts counts once.

2Check the data first

Almost every question today needs the same few facts about each order: whose it is, when it was, and which order it was for that customer (first, second, ...), with the dates of the orders either side of it. Day 13 rebuilt this as a CTE again and again. Today, save it once as a view, with Day 9's window functions:

Build 1: one row per order, numbered per customer
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW customer_orders AS
WITH orders AS (
    SELECT customer_id, order_id,
           MIN(order_date) AS order_date,
           SUM(line_total) AS revenue
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date < '2024-12-25'               -- settled data only
    GROUP BY customer_id, order_id
)
SELECT customer_id, order_id, order_date, revenue,
       ROW_NUMBER()     OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY order_date, order_id) AS order_number,
       LAG(order_date)  OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY order_date, order_id) AS previous_order_date,
       LEAD(order_date) OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY order_date, order_id) AS next_order_date
FROM orders;

clean_sales has one row per order line, so the CTE first gets back to one row per order (Day 10's grain). order_id breaks ties when two orders share a timestamp (Day 9). A customer's last order has no next order, so its next_order_date is NULL. Keep that in mind: it will matter in section 6.

Now a question nobody asked, but everyone should: do we see each customer's whole history? Our orders start on 1 January 2023. But some customers signed up before that. Compare them with everyone else:

SQL
SELECT CASE WHEN c.signup_date < '2023-01-01' THEN 'Signed up in 2022'
            ELSE 'Signed up in 2023 or 2024' END              AS signed_up,
       COUNT(*)                                               AS customers,
       ROUND(AVG(DATEDIFF(o.order_date, c.signup_date)))      AS avg_days_to_first_order,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(o.next_order_date IS NOT NULL), 1)     AS ordered_again_pct
FROM customer_orders o
JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.order_number = 1
GROUP BY signed_up;
Result
+---------------------------+-----------+-------------------------+-------------------+
| signed_up                 | customers | avg_days_to_first_order | ordered_again_pct |
+---------------------------+-----------+-------------------------+-------------------+
| Signed up in 2023 or 2024 |      1990 |                       5 |              36.1 |
| Signed up in 2022         |       119 |                     157 |              82.4 |
+---------------------------+-----------+-------------------------+-------------------+

Two warning signs. Most customers place their first order within days of signing up (five on average), but the 2022 sign-ups took five months on average. And over 80% of them ordered again, more than twice the usual rate. The simplest explanation: they were buying in 2022, and those orders aren't in our data. Their "first order" in our tables is really a later one, from a customer who was already loyal.

It's like walking into a film halfway through. A character you think has just arrived was there all along. Statisticians have names for this. These customers' real first orders are left-censored: we only know they happened before 2023, not when. And customers who bought only in 2022 are missing completely, which is called left truncation. Both come from the same thing: the data starts in the middle of some customers' stories. It flatters any analysis that starts at the "first" order. How much did it flatter Day 13's cohorts?

SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                            AS first_bought_in_2023_q1,
       SUM(c.signup_date < '2023-01-01')   AS signed_up_in_2022
FROM customer_orders o
JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.order_number = 1
  AND o.order_date < '2023-04-01';
Result
+-------------------------+-------------------+
| first_bought_in_2023_q1 | signed_up_in_2022 |
+-------------------------+-------------------+
|                     243 |                83 |
+-------------------------+-------------------+

A third of Day 13's 2023-Q1 cohort, the one it called "the best cohort", were these customers. Now we know why it looked best. The fix is simple: for anything that starts from the first order (cohorts, second orders), leave out customers who signed up before 2023. This also drops a few genuinely new customers who signed up late in 2022 and first bought in January 2023: a small price for a fair comparison. They're all real customers, though, so they stay in everything else: who's active today, and who to win back.

3Monthly cohorts

Day 13 followed each quarter's new customers. Monthly cohorts are the same idea at a finer grain, and they're what most businesses use. The month number trick is Day 13's, with 12 instead of 4: YEAR * 12 + MONTH puts all months on one line, so "months later" is a subtraction, even across New Year.

SQL
WITH first_months AS (
    SELECT o.customer_id,
           DATE_FORMAT(o.order_date, '%Y-%m')            AS cohort,
           YEAR(o.order_date) * 12 + MONTH(o.order_date) AS cohort_index
    FROM customer_orders o
    JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    WHERE o.order_number = 1
      AND c.signup_date >= '2023-01-01'                -- full history only (section 2)
),
activity AS (
    SELECT f.customer_id, f.cohort, f.cohort_index,
           YEAR(o.order_date) * 12 + MONTH(o.order_date) - f.cohort_index AS months_later
    FROM first_months f
    JOIN customer_orders o ON o.customer_id = f.customer_id
),
cohorts AS (
    SELECT cohort, cohort_index,
           COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)                                       AS customers,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = 1  THEN customer_id END)  AS m1,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = 2  THEN customer_id END)  AS m2,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = 3  THEN customer_id END)  AS m3,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = 6  THEN customer_id END)  AS m6,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = 12 THEN customer_id END)  AS m12
    FROM activity
    GROUP BY cohort, cohort_index
)
SELECT cohort, customers,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 1  <= 2024 * 12 + 11 THEN ROUND(100 * m1  / customers) END AS m_plus_1,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 2  <= 2024 * 12 + 11 THEN ROUND(100 * m2  / customers) END AS m_plus_2,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 3  <= 2024 * 12 + 11 THEN ROUND(100 * m3  / customers) END AS m_plus_3,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 6  <= 2024 * 12 + 11 THEN ROUND(100 * m6  / customers) END AS m_plus_6,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 12 <= 2024 * 12 + 11 THEN ROUND(100 * m12 / customers) END AS m_plus_12
FROM cohorts
ORDER BY cohort_index;

Each cell answers: "of the customers who first bought in this month, what share bought again exactly k months later?" The NULLs are Day 13's triangle: 2024 * 12 + 11 is November 2024, the last complete month, since December is only settled to the 24th. A cell for a month we haven't fully seen says "not known yet", never 0%.

Three things to notice:

  • The numbers are small. Most cells are between about 8% and 20%: only that share of a cohort buys in any single later month. That isn't as bad as it looks. Day 13 found 53 days on average between one order and the next (73 from the first to the second), so loyal customers simply skip some months. A single-month measure suits a phone contract, where people pay every month. For a shop, the wider windows in sections 4 and 6 tell you more.
  • The calendar shows through. Look for the high cells: October 2023's cohort one month later (November, Diwali), December 2023's one month later (January, with its New Year offer, the NEWYEAR10 coupon), July 2023's three months later (October). The May cohorts are weak in both years, because their next few months are the quiet summer. These are the calendar effects Day 13 told you to look for along the diagonals: the month a cohort lands in matters as much as the cohort.
  • Cohorts are small too, 45 to 111 customers. One extra person moves a cell by about 1 to 2 points, so don't compare single cells. Pool them instead.

Pooling means asking the question once for all customers: "of everyone who has been with us at least k months, what share bought in month k?" That gives the retention curve. The ages CTE is a small list of the months to show, and the join keeps only customers old enough for each one:

SQL
WITH first_months AS (
    SELECT o.customer_id,
           YEAR(o.order_date) * 12 + MONTH(o.order_date) AS cohort_index
    FROM customer_orders o
    JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    WHERE o.order_number = 1
      AND c.signup_date >= '2023-01-01'
),
activity AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT f.customer_id,
           YEAR(o.order_date) * 12 + MONTH(o.order_date) - f.cohort_index AS months_later
    FROM first_months f
    JOIN customer_orders o ON o.customer_id = f.customer_id
),
ages AS (
              SELECT 1 AS months_later
    UNION ALL SELECT 2  UNION ALL SELECT 3 UNION ALL SELECT 6
    UNION ALL SELECT 9  UNION ALL SELECT 12 UNION ALL SELECT 18
)
SELECT a.months_later,
       COUNT(*)                                       AS customers_old_enough,
       COUNT(x.customer_id)                           AS bought_that_month,
       ROUND(100 * COUNT(x.customer_id) / COUNT(*), 1) AS retention_pct
FROM ages a
JOIN first_months f    ON f.cohort_index + a.months_later <= 2024 * 12 + 11
LEFT JOIN activity x   ON x.customer_id = f.customer_id
                      AND x.months_later = a.months_later
GROUP BY a.months_later
ORDER BY a.months_later;
Result

| months_later | customers_old_enough | bought_that_month | retention_pct |
+--------------+----------------------+-------------------+---------------+
|            1 |                 1810 |               288 |          15.9 |
|            2 |                 1710 |               249 |          14.6 |
|            3 |                 1612 |               215 |          13.3 |
|            6 |                 1318 |               152 |          11.5 |
|            9 |                 1075 |               118 |          11.0 |
|           12 |                  870 |                70 |           8.0 |
|           18 |                  306 |                22 |           7.2 |
+--------------+----------------------+-------------------+---------------+

The curve has the classic shape: a fall, then a long, slow slide. About 16% of customers buy in the month after their first, 11 to 12% are still buying in a given month half a year later, and 7 to 8% a year and more later. It never reaches zero: there's a loyal core that keeps buying. Every business wants to lift the whole curve, but the early months are where most customers are lost, and so where a campaign can change the most.

The LEFT JOIN matters here: customers who didn't buy in month k still have to count in the denominator (Day 7). COUNT(x.customer_id) counts only the matches.

4When is a customer lost?

Every churn number depends on one choice: how long must someone be silent before we call them lost? Teams often pick 90 days, or a year, by habit. A better way is to test the rule on the past. Go back to a date where we can see what happened next, and ask: of the customers who had been silent for so many days, how many came back?

Our data allows six months of "what happened next" if we stand on 24 June 2024. So: for each customer who had ordered by then, how long had they been silent on that day, and did they order again in the six months to 24 December?

SQL
WITH on_24_june AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           DATEDIFF('2024-06-24', MAX(order_date)) AS days_silent,
           COUNT(*)                                AS orders_so_far
    FROM customer_orders
    WHERE order_date < '2024-06-25'
    GROUP BY customer_id
),
came_back AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_id
    FROM customer_orders
    WHERE order_date >= '2024-06-25'                   -- the next six months
)
SELECT CASE WHEN days_silent <= 30  THEN '1. Up to 30 days'
            WHEN days_silent <= 90  THEN '2. 31 to 90 days'
            WHEN days_silent <= 120 THEN '3. 91 to 120 days'
            WHEN days_silent <= 180 THEN '4. 121 to 180 days'
            WHEN days_silent <= 270 THEN '5. 181 to 270 days'
            WHEN days_silent <= 365 THEN '6. 271 to 365 days'
            ELSE                         '7. Over a year' END                    AS silent_for,
       COUNT(*)                                                                  AS customers,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(k.customer_id IS NOT NULL), 1)                            AS came_back_pct,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN orders_so_far = 1  THEN k.customer_id IS NOT NULL END), 1) AS one_order_pct,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN orders_so_far >= 2 THEN k.customer_id IS NOT NULL END), 1) AS two_plus_orders_pct
FROM on_24_june j
LEFT JOIN came_back k ON k.customer_id = j.customer_id
GROUP BY silent_for
ORDER BY silent_for;
Result
+--------------------+-----------+---------------+---------------+---------------------+
| silent_for         | customers | came_back_pct | one_order_pct | two_plus_orders_pct |
+--------------------+-----------+---------------+---------------+---------------------+
| 1. Up to 30 days   |       213 |          59.2 |          37.4 |                75.4 |
| 2. 31 to 90 days   |       273 |          42.5 |          17.3 |                66.4 |
| 3. 91 to 120 days  |        89 |          27.0 |           8.5 |                47.6 |
| 4. 121 to 180 days |       169 |          25.4 |          14.1 |                35.2 |
| 5. 181 to 270 days |       283 |           8.5 |           5.9 |                13.7 |
| 6. 271 to 365 days |       210 |           2.4 |           2.4 |                 2.3 |
| 7. Over a year     |       275 |           2.2 |           2.0 |                 3.6 |
+--------------------+-----------+---------------+---------------+---------------------+

This is the most useful table of the day. Read down came_back_pct: the longer the silence, the less likely a return, with a clear drop after 180 days. Customers silent for up to six months still came back about a quarter of the time or more. After six months, fewer than 1 in 10 did, and after nine months almost nobody. It's like deciding when a missing parcel is lost: wait until the point where parcels almost never turn up afterwards.

Now read across. A customer with two or more orders is far more likely to return than a one-time buyer silent for the same time. Silence means different things for different customers. (The CASE without ELSE gives NULL for the other group, and AVG skips it, Day 21's trick.)

So, the rule for today, written down where everyone can see it:

Status on 24 Dec 2024RuleWhat the past says
ActiveOrdered in the last 90 daysAbout half order again within six months
LapsingSilent for 91 to 180 daysAbout a quarter come back; more if they've ordered twice
ChurnedSilent for more than 180 daysFewer than 1 in 10 come back

Save it as a view, so every section and every future report uses the same rule:

Build 2: each customer's status on 24 December 2024
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW customer_status AS
SELECT customer_id,
       MIN(order_date)                         AS first_order_date,
       MAX(order_date)                         AS last_order_date,
       COUNT(*)                                AS orders,
       SUM(revenue)                            AS revenue,
       DATEDIFF('2024-12-24', MAX(order_date)) AS days_silent,
       CASE WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-24', MAX(order_date)) <= 90  THEN 'Active'
            WHEN DATEDIFF('2024-12-24', MAX(order_date)) <= 180 THEN 'Lapsing'
            ELSE 'Churned' END                 AS status
FROM customer_orders
GROUP BY customer_id;
SQL
SELECT status,
       COUNT(*)                                                 AS customers,
       SUM(orders >= 2)                                         AS repeat_buyers,
       ROUND(100 * COUNT(*) / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (), 1)         AS pct_of_customers,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(revenue) / SUM(SUM(revenue)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_lifetime_revenue
FROM customer_status
GROUP BY status
ORDER BY MIN(days_silent);
Result
+---------+-----------+---------------+------------------+-------------------------+
| status  | customers | repeat_buyers | pct_of_customers | pct_of_lifetime_revenue |
+---------+-----------+---------------+------------------+-------------------------+
| Active  |       665 |           415 |             31.5 |                    51.0 |
| Lapsing |       270 |            91 |             12.8 |                    10.8 |
| Churned |      1174 |           310 |             55.7 |                    38.2 |
+---------+-----------+---------------+------------------+-------------------------+

Day 13's percent-of-total pattern again (SUM(SUM(...)) OVER ()), and ORDER BY MIN(days_silent) puts the statuses in a sensible order without a sorting column. More than half of everyone who has ever bought is churned. That sounds alarming, but remember Day 13: 61% of customers buy once, and most one-time buyers end up here. Notice, though, the 310 churned repeat buyers: people who liked the shop enough to come back, and then stopped. And the 91 lapsing repeat buyers, who haven't gone yet.

5How many are we losing?

Farah's first question needs a rate over time. Think of the active customers as water in a tank. Each month, new customers pour in from one tap, returning customers from another, and some water leaks out as customers pass 180 days of silence. If the taps beat the leak, the tank fills. This is often called growth accounting, and it has a lovely property: the numbers must balance, so you can check your own SQL.

"Active" here must mean exactly what Build 2 means: silent for 180 days or less, counted with DATEDIFF on a given day. So for each month, measure it on two days: the last day of the previous month (the start) and the month's own last day (the end). For each month, and each customer who had ordered by its end, work out the yes-or-no facts with MAX over their orders (Day 6's 1-and-0 trick):

SQL
WITH months AS (
    SELECT cal_date                                      AS month_start,
           cal_date - INTERVAL 1 DAY                     AS day_before,   -- "start" of the month
           cal_date + INTERVAL 1 MONTH - INTERVAL 1 DAY  AS last_day      -- "end" of the month
    FROM calendar
    WHERE DAY(cal_date) = 1
      AND cal_date >= '2024-01-01' AND cal_date < '2024-12-01'
),
flags AS (
    SELECT m.month_start, o.customer_id,
           MAX(o.order_date < m.month_start
               AND DATEDIFF(m.day_before, o.order_date) <= 180) AS active_at_start,
           MAX(DATEDIFF(m.last_day, o.order_date) <= 180)       AS active_at_end,
           MAX(o.order_date >= m.month_start)                   AS bought_this_month,
           MIN(o.order_date) >= m.month_start                   AS is_new,
           COUNT(*)                                             AS orders_so_far
    FROM months m
    JOIN customer_orders o ON DATE(o.order_date) <= m.last_day
    GROUP BY m.month_start, o.customer_id
)
SELECT DATE_FORMAT(month_start, '%Y-%m')                                   AS month,
       SUM(active_at_start)                                                AS active_at_start,
       SUM(is_new)                                                         AS new_customers,
       SUM(bought_this_month AND NOT active_at_start AND NOT is_new)       AS came_back,
       SUM(active_at_start AND NOT active_at_end)                          AS churned,
       SUM(active_at_end)                                                  AS active_at_end,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(active_at_start AND NOT active_at_end)
                 / SUM(active_at_start), 1)                                AS churn_rate_pct,
       SUM(active_at_start AND NOT active_at_end AND orders_so_far = 1)    AS churned_after_one_order
FROM flags
GROUP BY month_start
ORDER BY month_start;

How it works: the join gives each month every order placed up to its last day, so MIN(order_date) is the customer's first order and COUNT(*) their orders so far. "Churned" means active at the start, not at the end: their 180 days ran out during the month. "Came back" means a customer who had already churned, ordering again. One detail: it's tempting to write the window as order_date >= month_start - INTERVAL 180 DAY, but that is a 179-day rule in DATEDIFF terms, one day different from Build 2. Off-by-one differences like this are why two "churn" numbers from the same data often disagree slightly. Use the same expression everywhere.

Now check that it balances, using January: 785 active at the start, plus 47 new, plus 8 who came back, minus 67 churned, gives 773, exactly the number active at the end. Every row balances like this. If yours doesn't, a condition is wrong. That check is worth more than any amount of staring at the query.

What the table says:

  • The tank is filling. 785 active customers at the start of 2024, 926 by the end of November. The shop loses about 1 in 10 of its active customers each month, and wins more than that back with new customers.
  • Almost all the growth comes from the new-customer tap. Customers who come back after churning are a trickle, 2 to 16 a month, which is what section 4 predicted.
  • Most of the leak is one-time buyers: across the year, about two-thirds of those who churned had placed a single order (591 of 907).
  • Churn lags. A customer is counted as churned 180 days after their last order, so this month's churn is really about six months ago. April and May 2024 have the highest churn of the year: those are customers whose last order was between early October and early December 2023, the festive season. A busy season brings a wave of churn half a year later, simply because it brought a wave of new customers, most of whom buy once. Don't read it as "something went wrong in May".

6What brings a customer back?

The biggest leak is after the first order, so the obvious next question is: what about the first order predicts a second? Day 20 suggested one idea to check: "did the festive coupon bring in bargain hunters who never return?" Others are easy to imagine. Do customers who used the welcome coupon come back less? Do customers who returned something come back less?

Use Day 13's fair measure: a second order within 90 days, only for customers whose first order was at least 90 days before our "today" (before 26 September 2024), and only customers with full history (section 2). Then stack the answers to several questions in one table with UNION ALL (Day 16):

SQL
WITH firsts AS (
    SELECT o.customer_id, o.order_id, o.order_date,
           COALESCE(o.next_order_date < o.order_date + INTERVAL 90 DAY, 0) AS back_within_90
    FROM customer_orders o
    JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    WHERE o.order_number = 1
      AND o.order_date < '2024-09-26'                -- everyone had 90 days
      AND c.signup_date >= '2023-01-01'              -- full history
),
details AS (
    SELECT f.customer_id, f.back_within_90,
           COALESCE(MAX(s.coupon_code), 'No coupon')                      AS coupon,
           MAX(s.channel)                                                 AS channel,
           MAX(r.return_id IS NOT NULL)                                   AS returned_something,
           SUM(s.line_total)                                              AS order_value,
           DATE(f.order_date) IN (SELECT sale_day FROM festive_sale_days) AS on_sale_day
    FROM firsts f
    JOIN clean_sales s        ON s.order_id = f.order_id
    LEFT JOIN clean_returns r ON r.order_item_id = s.order_item_id
    GROUP BY f.customer_id, f.order_date, f.back_within_90
),
stacked AS (
              SELECT 0 AS q, 'All first orders' AS question, 'Everyone' AS answer,
                     COUNT(*) AS customers, AVG(back_within_90) AS rate
              FROM details
    UNION ALL SELECT 1, 'Coupon used', coupon, COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY coupon
    UNION ALL SELECT 2, 'Ordered on', channel, COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY channel
    UNION ALL SELECT 3, 'On a festive sale day', IF(on_sale_day, 'Yes', 'No') AS a3,
                     COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY a3
    UNION ALL SELECT 4, 'Returned something', IF(returned_something, 'Yes', 'No') AS a4,
                     COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY a4
    UNION ALL SELECT 5, 'First order value',
                     CASE WHEN order_value < 1000 THEN 'Under 1,000'
                          WHEN order_value < 2500 THEN '1,000 to 2,499'
                          ELSE '2,500 or more' END AS a5,
                     COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY a5
    UNION ALL SELECT 6, 'Customer ID is', IF(MOD(customer_id, 2) = 0, 'Even', 'Odd') AS a6,
                     COUNT(*), AVG(back_within_90)
              FROM details GROUP BY a6
)
SELECT question, answer, customers,
       ROUND(100 * rate, 1)                                             AS back_within_90_pct,
       CAST(200 * SQRT(rate * (1 - rate) / customers) AS DECIMAL(4,1)) AS give_or_take
FROM stacked
ORDER BY q, customers DESC;

Two things in this query need explaining. The last question is a deliberate joke: odd or even customer IDs. An ID is just a label, so whether it's odd or even can't possibly affect whether someone comes back. (MOD(x, 2) is the remainder after dividing by 2, so it's 0 for even numbers.) Any difference between odd and even is pure luck. A check like this is called a placebo test: it shows you what luck looks like in your own data. Here odd and even differ by 1 point. But that's just one roll of the dice; another split could easily have given 3 or 4 points. To know how big luck can get, you need the next column.

That's the give_or_take column, a rough rule from statistics. If a group of n customers came back at rate p, then 2 × √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n) is about how far that rate could move by luck alone (roughly a 95% range). Big groups give small ranges, small groups big ones. To compare two groups, combine their ranges: the range for the difference is about √(a² + b²), where a and b are the two groups' give-or-takes. A difference smaller than that could easily be luck. For the odd and even IDs, that range is about √(3.1² + 3.1²) ≈ 4.4 points, comfortably bigger than the 1-point gap luck happened to give. Two more cautions. The rule needs at least about 10 customers who came back and 10 who didn't in a group; below that, just say "too few to tell". And when you check many groups at once, about 1 gap in 20 will cross the line by luck alone, so one surprising result among many deserves a second look before anyone acts on it.

Now read the table with that ruler:

  • Welcome coupon: 31.4% against 26.9%, a gap of 4.5 points. The range for the difference is about √(4.7² + 2.5²) ≈ 5.3. So the coupon might help a little, but this data can't tell.
  • Festive sale day: if anything, the 74 customers who first bought in a sale came back more often, not less. But 74 is a small group (give or take 11 points), so the honest answer to Day 20's idea is "no sign of bargain hunters, and too few to be sure".
  • App or website, a return on the first order, order size: every gap is inside its range. The FESTIVE15 and NEWYEAR10 rows are too small to read at all.

This is a perfectly good finding, and an important one: none of the first-order details we tested clearly predicts a second order. (That's not the same as "nothing matters": a small effect could hide inside these ranges, and there are details we didn't test.) It saves Farah from building a campaign on a pattern that isn't there. In real work, "we checked, and it doesn't matter" is reported just as plainly as "it matters".

What does predict it: the orders already placed

If the first order's details don't tell you much, what does? The customer's own behaviour. Here's the chance of another order within 90 days, after each order number. The grain is orders now: a customer with six orders appears in several rows.

Once a customer has cleared it, they're likely to keep going. That's why so many shops focus their effort on the first 30 to 90 days (Day 13's advice for its "New" segment). One caution in reading it: this doesn't prove that a second order makes people loyal. Partly, the customers who place a second order were the loyal kind all along, and the second order just reveals them. Either way, it's the best signal the shop has of who will stay.

Why the COALESCE? It's Day 20's trap 1. A customer's last order has no next order, so next_order_date < ... is NULL, and AVG would skip those orders entirely. Without COALESCE(..., 0), the first row would say 72% instead of 28%: it would average only over the customers who came back at all. Always ask what a NULL means before you average over it.

Make the campaign measurable

Here's a trap that catches whole marketing teams. Say Farah emails group 1, and 40% of them buy in the next three months. Success? Section 4 says many of them would have come back anyway. To know what the campaign added, you need a holdout group: a random slice of the list that gets nothing, and is measured the same way. Doctors do the same when they test a medicine, by comparing against patients who didn't get it.

The slice must be random, but repeatable, so that anyone can rebuild it later. A hash does that. CRC32 turns any value into a scrambled number, always the same for the same input, and MOD(..., 5) takes the remainder after dividing by 5, which is 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4. So MOD(CRC32(customer_id), 5) = 0 picks a fixed, effectively random fifth of the customers:

SQL
SELECT s.customer_id,
       CONCAT(c.first_name, ' ', c.last_name)                       AS name,
       s.orders,
       ROUND(s.revenue)                                             AS lifetime_spend,
       s.days_silent,
       IF(MOD(CRC32(s.customer_id), 5) = 0, 'Hold out', 'Contact')  AS campaign_group
FROM customer_status s
JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = s.customer_id
WHERE s.orders >= 2
  AND s.days_silent BETWEEN 91 AND 270
ORDER BY s.revenue DESC
LIMIT 10;

       MIN(o.order_date) AS ordered_again_on,
       MIN(o.status)     AS status_now
FROM customer_status s
JOIN clean_orders o ON o.customer_id = s.customer_id
WHERE s.orders >= 2
  AND s.days_silent BETWEEN 91 AND 270
  AND o.order_date >= '2024-12-25'
  AND o.is_test = 0 AND o.is_duplicate = 0

🎯 Interview corner

Retention questions are favourites in analyst interviews, because they test definitions and judgment as much as SQL. Expect at least one "how would you define churn?"

Concept questions

Q1How would you define churn for a shop with no subscriptions?

From silence: no order for a set number of days. Choose the number with data: go back to a past date, group customers by how long they'd been silent, and see what share came back afterwards. Pick the point where returns become rare. Write the rule down next to every churn number, because a different rule gives a different number.

Q2What's the difference between a repeat rate, cohort retention and a churn rate?

A repeat rate is the share of customers who ever ordered twice (fair only within a fixed window). Cohort retention follows a group who started together and shows what share is still buying 1, 2, 3 ... months later. A churn rate is the share of currently active customers who become inactive in a period. Each answers a different question.

Q3What are left-censoring and left truncation, and why do they matter for cohorts?

The data starts in the middle of some customers' history. For customers who were already buying, the real first order is left-censored: we know it was before the data starts, not when. Customers who stopped before the data starts are missing entirely: that's left truncation. Either way, the first order in the data isn't the real first order, so long-standing customers look like new customers who are unusually loyal, which inflates early cohorts. Leave out customers you can't see from the start (for example, those who signed up before the data begins).

Q4Why does a win-back campaign need a holdout group?

Because some customers would have come back anyway. Without a group that received nothing, you can't separate the campaign's effect from normal returns. The holdout must be random (a hash of the customer ID is repeatable), measured the same way, and big enough to show the effect you care about.

Q5Churn jumped this month. Does that mean something went wrong this month?

Not necessarily. With a silence-based rule, churn is recorded months after the last order, so it reflects who was buying back then. A big season produces a wave of churn later, mostly one-time buyers. Check when the churned customers last ordered before looking for a cause this month.

Q6A segment comes back at 31% and another at 27%. Is the first better?

Maybe, maybe not. Check whether the gap is bigger than chance: roughly 2 × √(p(1−p)/n) for each group, combined for the difference. A placebo split (such as odd and even IDs) shows what a pure-luck difference looks like in your data, but it's a single example, so use the formula for the size. With small groups, say "can't tell" rather than picking a winner.

Practical questions

P1Sketch the SQL for a monthly cohort retention table.

Find each customer's first order month. Join back to all their orders and compute months_later as (YEAR*12 + MONTH) of the order minus that of the first order. Group by cohort, with COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN months_later = k THEN customer_id END) for each k, divided by the cohort size. Return NULL for cells beyond the last complete month.

P2Your query says 72% of customers order again within 90 days. It seems high. What would you check?

Whether customers with no next order are being dropped. If the "next order" is NULL for them, a comparison gives NULL and AVG skips them, so the average covers only customers who came back at some point. Wrap the condition in COALESCE(..., 0). Also check the fair window: only customers with at least 90 days of history should count.

P3The win-back email went to 1,000 customers and 30% bought within a month. The holdout of 250 bought at 26%. What do you report?

The campaign's effect is the difference, about 4 points (around 40 extra customers), not 30%. Then check it against chance: about ±2.9 points for the 1,000 and ±5.5 for the 250, so about ±6 for the difference. A 4-point gap is within that, so the effect isn't proven yet. Report it as "possibly positive, needs another round".

Quick recap

  • Check whether you see each customer's whole history. Customers who were buying before the data starts look like unusually loyal new customers (left-censoring): leave them out of anything that starts from the first order.
  • Monthly cohorts use YEAR * 12 + MONTH, NULL for months not yet seen, and are small and noisy for a shop this size. Pool them into a retention curve.
  • For a shop, churn is defined by silence. Test the rule on the past: here, after 180 days fewer than 1 in 10 came back. Save the rule as a view.
  • Growth accounting (start + new + came back − churned = end) must balance, which checks your SQL. Churn lags its cause by the length of the rule.
  • None of the first-order details we tested clearly predicted a second order here. Use a placebo split and a give-or-take of 2 × √(p(1−p)/n) before believing a gap. The orders already placed are the strong signal: the second order is the hurdle.
  • Target customers who are slipping now, not those long gone, and keep a random, repeatable holdout (MOD(CRC32(id), 5)) so the campaign can be measured.
