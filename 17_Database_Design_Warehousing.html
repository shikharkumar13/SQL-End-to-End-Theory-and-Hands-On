1Two jobs, two designs

Think of a library. The checkout desk handles a steady stream of tiny jobs: lend this book, take that one back, renew a third. Each job must be quick and exactly right, because two people can't borrow the same copy. Once a year, the head librarian writes a report: which subjects are borrowed most, which branches are busiest, how borrowing changed over five years. That job reads everything, rarely changes anything, and doesn't mind waiting a minute.

Databases face the same two jobs, and they have names:

OLTP: running the businessOLAP: analysing the business
Stands forOnline transaction processingOnline analytical processing
Typical workPlace an order, update an address, record a paymentRevenue by month and category, for three years
Rows per queryA fewMillions
WritesConstant, by the appIn batches, often once a night
Design goalEvery fact stored once, so updates are safeEasy, fast reading and summarising
Usual designNormalised (sections 2 and 3)Star schema (sections 5 and 6)
ExamplesMySQL or PostgreSQL behind an appBigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, or MySQL used as a warehouse

online_store_full is an OLTP design: it's how the shop's website and app store their data. Day 11's clean views were a first step towards an analytics layer on top of it. Today's last step is a proper warehouse.

2Relationships and keys

Designing a database starts with two questions: what are the things (customers, products, orders), and how are they related? Every relationship between two tables is one of three kinds:

KindMeaningIn the shopHow it's built
One-to-manyOne row on one side, any number on the otherOne customer has many orders. One order has many lines.A foreign key on the "many" side: orders.customer_id
Many-to-manyMany on both sidesAn order holds many products, and a product appears in many ordersA third table in between, holding a key to each side: order_items
One-to-oneAt most one on each sideRare. For example, a customer and their loyalty-card settingsA foreign key that is also unique (or shares the primary key)

The table in the middle of a many-to-many relationship is called a junction table (or bridge table). order_items is exactly that, and it carries facts that belong to the pair: how many of this product in this order, and at what price. You can check the relationships in the data itself:

SQL
SELECT 'orders per customer' AS relationship, MIN(n) AS fewest, MAX(n) AS most
FROM (SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) AS n FROM orders GROUP BY customer_id) AS t
UNION ALL
SELECT 'lines per order', MIN(n), MAX(n)
FROM (SELECT order_id, COUNT(*) AS n FROM order_items GROUP BY order_id) AS t
UNION ALL
SELECT 'orders per product', MIN(n), MAX(n)
FROM (SELECT product_id, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS n FROM order_items GROUP BY product_id) AS t;
Result
+---------------------+--------+------+
| relationship        | fewest | most |
+---------------------+--------+------+
| orders per customer |      1 |   44 |
| lines per order     |      1 |    4 |
| orders per product  |     10 |  481 |
+---------------------+--------+------+

One customer with 44 orders, an order with 4 lines, a product in 481 orders: all "many". (And remember Noah from Day 7: a customer can also have zero orders. "One-to-many" really means "one to zero or more", which is why LEFT JOIN matters.)

Choosing keys

A natural key is something real that seems unique: an email, a phone number, a PAN number. A surrogate key is a number the database makes up, like customer_id, which means nothing outside the database. Natural keys look tempting, but real data breaks them. Is email unique in our shop?

SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                                      AS accounts,
       COUNT(DISTINCT email COLLATE utf8mb4_0900_bin) AS exact_spellings,
       COUNT(DISTINCT LOWER(TRIM(email)))             AS real_people
FROM customers;
Result
+----------+-----------------+-------------+
| accounts | exact_spellings | real_people |
+----------+-----------------+-------------+
|     3028 |            3028 |        3003 |
+----------+-----------------+-------------+

Every account has a different email if you compare them exactly (with the case-sensitive collation from Day 10), so a strict check would have passed. But there are only 3,003 real people. The 25 duplicates from Day 10 differ only by capitals or spaces. People also change emails, share them, and mistype them. That's why the shop uses a surrogate key for identity. The good habit: a surrogate key as the primary key, plus a UNIQUE rule on a tidied natural value, such as the lower-case, trimmed email, to stop duplicates at the door.

Sometimes the key is two columns together, a composite key. Day 14's marketing_spend used (month_start, channel), because one channel has one spend figure per month. A junction table often works the same way: one row per order and product.

3Normalisation

Day 1 gave you the idea in one sentence: store each fact once, in one place, and connect tables with keys. It promised that formal rules exist. Here they are, starting with what goes wrong without them.

The problem: anomalies

Imagine the shop kept one flat table of orders, with the customer's city copied onto every order. Let's build that for Kavya (customer 1) as a temporary table, so nothing real changes, and then update her city on just one row, as a careless program might:

SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE flat_orders AS
SELECT o.order_id, o.order_date, c.customer_id, c.first_name, c.city
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE c.customer_id = 1;

UPDATE flat_orders SET city = 'Pune' WHERE order_id = 5129;

SELECT first_name, city, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM flat_orders
GROUP BY first_name, city;
Result
+------------+-----------+--------+
| first_name | city      | orders |
+------------+-----------+--------+
| Kavya      | Hyderabad |      6 |
| Kavya      | Pune      |      1 |
+------------+-----------+--------+

Where does Kavya live? The table now says two different things, and nothing can tell you which is right. That's an update anomaly: one fact stored in many places, changed in some of them. Flat tables have two more problems:

  • Insert anomaly: you can't record a new customer until they've placed an order, because there's no order row to put them in.
  • Delete anomaly: delete someone's only order, and you've deleted everything you knew about them.

In the real online_store_full, the city lives once, in customers. One update fixes it everywhere, which is the whole point.

The three normal forms

Normalisation is usually taught as a series of normal forms. Each one is a question you ask about a table. The first three cover almost everything you'll meet in practice.

First normal form (1NF): one value per cell. No lists inside a column, and no repeated columns like phone1, phone2, phone3. Here's why. Suppose product tags were stored as a comma-separated list:

SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE product_tags (product_name VARCHAR(50), tags VARCHAR(200));

INSERT INTO product_tags VALUES
    ('Yoga Mat',       'fitness,home,gift'),
    ('Cotton T-Shirt', 'clothing,outfit,gift'),
    ('Smartwatch',     'fitness,electronics,gift');

SELECT product_name
FROM product_tags
WHERE tags LIKE '%fit%';      -- "products tagged fitness"?
Result
+----------------+
| product_name   |
+----------------+
| Yoga Mat       |
| Cotton T-Shirt |
| Smartwatch     |
+----------------+

The T-shirt was found, because "outfit" contains "fit". Searching inside lists is fragile, you can't put a foreign key on a list, counting how many products have each tag needs tricks, and an index can't help. The 1NF design is a separate table with one row per product and tag: product_tag (product_id, tag), another junction table.

Second normal form (2NF): every column depends on the whole key. This only matters for composite keys. Imagine order_items with the key (order_id, product_id) also stored product_name. The name depends only on product_id, half of the key, so it would be copied onto every line that sells that product. Change a product's name, and you're back to the update anomaly. The name belongs in products, where it is.

Third normal form (3NF): no column depends on another non-key column. Imagine customers stored both city and state. The state depends on the city (Pune is always in Maharashtra), not on the customer. Store it once per city, in a table of cities. Day 11's city_lookup was exactly this kind of table.

One sentence to remember all three. Every column should depend on the key (1NF: there is a key, and one value per cell), the whole key (2NF), and nothing but the key (3NF). Higher forms (BCNF, 4NF, 5NF) exist and deal with rarer situations. In interviews, knowing the first three well, with an example of each, is what counts.

Is online_store_full normalised? Mostly, yes, to 3NF. But what about order_items.unit_price, when products already has a price? Day 2 answered that: today's price and the price paid on the day are two different facts, so storing both isn't a violation. Asking "is this really the same fact?" is the heart of design.

4When to break the rules

Normalisation makes writing safe: every fact has one home. But it makes reading work harder. Every report on our shop needs four or five joins before it can answer anything. For a busy app that's a fine trade. For analysts who only read, it's a lot of repeated effort. So analytics often denormalises on purpose: it stores data in fewer, wider tables, accepting repetition in exchange for simpler and faster reading.

NormalisedDenormalised
Each fact storedOncePossibly many times
Writing and updatingSafe and simpleRisky: copies can disagree
ReadingMany joinsFew joins, simpler queries
Best forThe app that runs the businessReports, dashboards, analysis

You've already done the safest kind of denormalisation. clean_sales (Day 11) is one wide row per order line, with the product name, category and customer all on it. But it's a view: the data still lives once, in the normalised tables, and the view joins it fresh every time. Copies can't disagree, because there are no copies.

The other kind stores the wide data for real, in a separate place that is only ever written by one controlled process, usually overnight. Then it's safe again: nobody updates the copies by hand. That's what a data warehouse is.

5Build a star schema

The most common warehouse design is the star schema. It has two kinds of tables:

  • A fact table records events, one row per event, with the numbers you'll add up: quantities, revenue, cost. Facts are the verbs: "this customer bought this product on this day".
  • Dimension tables describe the things involved, with the words you'll filter and group by: product names and categories, customer cities, dates and weekdays. Dimensions are the nouns.

Draw it, and the fact table sits in the middle with the dimensions around it, like the points of a star:

Fact: one row per order line, with the numbers (measures)Dimensions: who, what and when, with the descriptions

The very first design decision is the fact table's grain: what one row means (Day 10). Ours is "one product in one order", one row per order line, the most detailed level we have. Pick the most detailed grain you can. You can always add up later, but you can never split a total back into its parts.

The dimensions

A separate database keeps the warehouse apart from the shop, as it would be in a real company. First the date dimension. Day 12 promised this name: it's the calendar table, with a whole-number key like 20241119, which is short, sorts correctly, and is easy to read:

Build 1: the warehouse and dim_date
DROP DATABASE IF EXISTS shop_warehouse;
CREATE DATABASE shop_warehouse;
USE shop_warehouse;

CREATE TABLE dim_date (
    date_key    INT        PRIMARY KEY,   -- e.g. 20241119
    full_date   DATE       NOT NULL,
    year        SMALLINT   NOT NULL,
    quarter     TINYINT    NOT NULL,
    month       TINYINT    NOT NULL,
    month_name  CHAR(3)    NOT NULL,
    day_name    VARCHAR(9) NOT NULL,
    is_weekend  TINYINT    NOT NULL
);

INSERT INTO dim_date
SELECT CAST(DATE_FORMAT(cal_date, '%Y%m%d') AS UNSIGNED),
       cal_date, cal_year, cal_quarter, cal_month,
       DATE_FORMAT(cal_date, '%b'), day_name, is_weekend
FROM online_store_full.calendar;

online_store_full.calendar reads a table from another database: database name, a dot, then table name. Next, the customer and product dimensions:

Build 2: dim_customer and dim_product
CREATE TABLE dim_customer (
    customer_key        INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,   -- the warehouse's own key
    customer_id         INT NOT NULL,                     -- the shop's ID, kept for reference
    first_name          VARCHAR(50),
    last_name           VARCHAR(50),
    city                VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL,
    country             VARCHAR(50),
    acquisition_channel VARCHAR(30) NOT NULL,
    signup_date         DATE
);

INSERT INTO dim_customer (customer_id, first_name, last_name, city, country,
                          acquisition_channel, signup_date)
SELECT customer_id, first_name, last_name,
       COALESCE(city, 'Not given'), country,
       COALESCE(acquisition_channel, 'Not recorded'), signup_date
FROM online_store_full.clean_customers
WHERE customer_id = master_customer_id      -- one row per real person
  AND is_test = 0;                          -- no test accounts

CREATE TABLE dim_product (
    product_key   INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    product_id    INT NOT NULL,
    product_name  VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
    category_name VARCHAR(50)  NOT NULL,     -- copied in: no separate category table
    list_price    DECIMAL(10,2),
    is_active     TINYINT
);

INSERT INTO dim_product (product_id, product_name, category_name, list_price, is_active)
SELECT p.product_id, p.product_name, COALESCE(c.category_name, 'Uncategorised'),
       p.price, p.is_active
FROM online_store_full.products p
LEFT JOIN online_store_full.categories c ON c.category_id = p.category_id;

Three warehouse habits are visible here:

  • Built from the clean layer. Day 11's decisions (master IDs, no test accounts, fixed spellings) come along automatically.
  • No NULLs in the words people group by. A missing city becomes 'Not given', so every report shows the same honest label instead of a blank (Day 15's NULL "city" trap, fixed once at the source).
  • Surrogate keys. The warehouse makes its own keys (customer_key) and keeps the shop's ID beside them. Section 7 shows why.

Notice also that category_name is copied into dim_product: denormalised on purpose, so nobody needs a fifth join. Keeping a separate category table would make it a snowflake schema, where dimensions branch into more tables. Snowflakes save a little space, and stars save a lot of joins. Most teams choose the star.

The fact table

Build 3: fact_sales
CREATE TABLE fact_sales (
    sales_key    INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    date_key     INT NOT NULL,
    customer_key INT NOT NULL,
    product_key  INT NOT NULL,
    order_id     INT NOT NULL,          -- kept to count orders
    channel      VARCHAR(10),
    quantity     INT NOT NULL,
    revenue      DECIMAL(12,2) NOT NULL,
    cost         DECIMAL(12,2),
    FOREIGN KEY (date_key)     REFERENCES dim_date (date_key),
    FOREIGN KEY (customer_key) REFERENCES dim_customer (customer_key),
    FOREIGN KEY (product_key)  REFERENCES dim_product (product_key)
);

INSERT INTO fact_sales (date_key, customer_key, product_key, order_id, channel,
                        quantity, revenue, cost)
SELECT CAST(DATE_FORMAT(s.order_date, '%Y%m%d') AS UNSIGNED),
       dc.customer_key,
       dp.product_key,
       s.order_id,
       s.channel,
       s.quantity,
       s.line_total,
       s.line_cost
FROM online_store_full.clean_sales s
JOIN dim_customer dc ON dc.customer_id = s.customer_id
JOIN dim_product  dp ON dp.product_id  = s.product_id;

The two joins are the key lookup: for every sale, find the warehouse key of its customer and its product. order_id stays in the fact table with no dimension of its own, because there's nothing else to describe about an order here. Warehouse people call that a degenerate dimension. Now the most important check of any load: did everything arrive?

SQL
SELECT 'dim_date' AS table_name, COUNT(*) AS row_count FROM dim_date
UNION ALL SELECT 'dim_customer', COUNT(*) FROM dim_customer
UNION ALL SELECT 'dim_product',  COUNT(*) FROM dim_product
UNION ALL SELECT 'fact_sales',   COUNT(*) FROM fact_sales
UNION ALL SELECT 'revenue in fact_sales', ROUND(SUM(revenue)) FROM fact_sales;
Result
+-----------------------+-----------+
| table_name            | row_count |
+-----------------------+-----------+
| dim_date              |      1461 |
| dim_customer          |      3000 |
| dim_product           |        61 |
| fact_sales            |      9508 |
| revenue in fact_sales |  13172860 |
+-----------------------+-----------+

9,508 fact rows, the same as clean_sales, and ₹1,31,72,860, the same revenue as Day 11, to the rupee. If any sale had failed its key lookup (a customer missing from dim_customer, say), the inner joins would have silently dropped it, and this total would be lower. That's why every warehouse load ends by reconciling against the source. The 3,000 customers are the 3,028 accounts minus 25 duplicate accounts and 3 test accounts.

6Querying the star

Every question on a star schema has the same shape: start from the fact table, join the dimensions you need, filter and group by their words, and add up the fact's numbers. Do weekend shoppers favour some categories?

SQL
SELECT p.category_name,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN d.is_weekend = 1 THEN f.revenue ELSE 0 END) * 100
             / SUM(f.revenue), 1) AS weekend_pct_of_revenue
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_date d    ON d.date_key    = f.date_key
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
GROUP BY p.category_name
ORDER BY weekend_pct_of_revenue DESC;
Result
+----------------+------------------------+
| category_name  | weekend_pct_of_revenue |
+----------------+------------------------+
| Clothing       |                   34.2 |
| Electronics    |                   33.4 |
| Home & Kitchen |                   31.9 |
| Sports         |                   31.2 |
| Books          |                   30.2 |
| Uncategorised  |                   20.0 |
+----------------+------------------------+

Weekends are 2 days out of 7, about 28.6% of all days, and every main category gets more than that share of its revenue at weekends, with clothing the most (34%). (The Smart Fitness Band's 20% is based on one month of sales, too few to read anything into.) On the shop's own tables, this question would need orders, order_items, products, categories, the cleaning rules, and date functions. Here, it's two joins and a ready-made is_weekend column.

A second example: the top cities in 2024.

SQL
SELECT c.city,
       ROUND(SUM(f.revenue)) AS revenue_2024
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_date d     ON d.date_key     = f.date_key
JOIN dim_customer c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key
WHERE d.year = 2024
  AND c.city <> 'Not given'
GROUP BY c.city
ORDER BY revenue_2024 DESC
LIMIT 3;
Result
+-----------+--------------+
| city      | revenue_2024 |
+-----------+--------------+
| Delhi     |       763594 |
| Mumbai    |       759343 |
| Bengaluru |       739125 |
+-----------+--------------+

The same shape again. That sameness is the real power of a star: BI tools like Power BI and Tableau are built around it. Point them at a fact table and its dimensions, and people who don't write SQL can drag "city" and "year" onto a chart and get the right numbers. The warehouse does the hard thinking once, so every report doesn't have to.

Most fact tables also have summary versions. A dashboard that only ever shows monthly revenue by category doesn't need to add up 9,508 rows every time. Teams often build aggregate tables from the fact table (one row per month and category, say) and refresh them in the same nightly load. It's the same trade-off as section 4: faster reading, in exchange for copies that must be rebuilt.

7When dimensions change

Kavya lives in Hyderabad. In July 2024, she moves to Pune. What should the warehouse do? This is the classic slowly changing dimension (SCD) problem: descriptions change slowly, but they do change, and reports about the past depend on what you do.

  • Type 1: overwrite. Change her city to Pune. Simple, but history is rewritten: her 2023 orders now count as Pune sales, although she lived in Hyderabad then.
  • Type 2: add a new row. Keep the old row, mark it as ended, and add a new row with a new surrogate key, valid from the move date. Old facts keep pointing at the Hyderabad row, and new facts at the Pune row. History stays true.
  • Type 3: add a column. Keep previous_city next to city. Only one step of history. Rarely used.

Type 2 is the one interviewers ask about, and it's why warehouses use surrogate keys: one customer can now have two rows, so customer_id alone can't be the key. Here's how it looks, in a temporary table:

SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE dim_customer_history (
    customer_key INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    customer_id  INT NOT NULL,
    first_name   VARCHAR(50),
    city         VARCHAR(50),
    valid_from   DATE NOT NULL,
    valid_to     DATE NOT NULL,
    is_current   TINYINT NOT NULL
);

-- Kavya as she was first loaded
INSERT INTO dim_customer_history (customer_id, first_name, city, valid_from, valid_to, is_current)
VALUES (1, 'Kavya', 'Hyderabad', '2023-11-02', '9999-12-31', 1);

-- She moves on 1 July 2024: close the old row, then add the new one
UPDATE dim_customer_history
SET valid_to = '2024-06-30', is_current = 0
WHERE customer_id = 1 AND is_current = 1;

INSERT INTO dim_customer_history (customer_id, first_name, city, valid_from, valid_to, is_current)
VALUES (1, 'Kavya', 'Pune', '2024-07-01', '9999-12-31', 1);

SELECT * FROM dim_customer_history;
Result
+--------------+-------------+------------+-----------+------------+------------+------------+
| customer_key | customer_id | first_name | city      | valid_from | valid_to   | is_current |
+--------------+-------------+------------+-----------+------------+------------+------------+
|            1 |           1 | Kavya      | Hyderabad | 2023-11-02 | 2024-06-30 |          0 |
|            2 |           1 | Kavya      | Pune      | 2024-07-01 | 9999-12-31 |          1 |
+--------------+-------------+------------+-----------+------------+------------+------------+

Two rows for one person, each with its own key and its own period. '9999-12-31' is a common convention meaning "still true". When a new order arrives, the load looks up the row that's current on the order date: WHERE customer_id = 1 AND order_date BETWEEN valid_from AND valid_to (safe with BETWEEN, because these are DATE columns, Day 12). Her May 2024 order gets key 1 (Hyderabad), and her August 2024 order gets key 2 (Pune). A report of revenue by city is then right for every period.

Our warehouse uses Type 1, which is fine here, because the shop's data has no history of changes to keep. Choosing the type is a business question: "when a customer moves, should their past sales move with them?"

8How data reaches the warehouse

You just did by hand what companies do automatically every night. The process has a name: ETL, for extract (copy data out of the source systems), transform (clean it and reshape it into facts and dimensions), and load (write it into the warehouse). Your own steps map onto it exactly:

StepWhat it meansWhere you did it
ExtractGet the data out of the app's database, files and other toolsDay 10's online_store_full, Day 14's CSV
TransformClean, fix, deduplicate, apply business rulesDay 11's clean layer
LoadBuild the facts and dimensions, and reconcile the totalsToday, sections 5 and 6

Modern cloud warehouses are so powerful that many teams now load the raw data first and transform it inside the warehouse with SQL. That's called ELT (the same letters, a different order). A tool called dbt is widely used for it: each transformation is a SQL SELECT saved in a file, much like Day 11's views, and dbt runs them in the right order, tests them, and documents them. The people who build these models are often called analytics engineers, and it's a natural next step for analysts who enjoyed Days 10, 11 and 17.

Two more reasons warehouses are fast for analysis. Many store data by column rather than by row, so adding up revenue reads only that column, not every column of every row. And they spread big queries across many machines at once. You don't need to manage either as an analyst. But it explains why a query that's slow on the shop's MySQL can take seconds in BigQuery, and why "only select the columns you need" (Day 5) saves real money there, since many cloud warehouses charge by the amount of data a query reads.

3.

SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE wishlist (
    customer_id INT      NOT NULL,
    product_id  INT      NOT NULL,
    added_at    DATETIME NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, product_id)    -- one row per customer and product
);

INSERT INTO wishlist VALUES
    (1, 14, '2024-12-01 10:00:00'),
    (1,  8, '2024-12-01 10:05:00'),
    (2, 14, '2024-12-02 18:30:00');

INSERT INTO wishlist VALUES (1, 14, '2024-12-03 09:00:00');   -- the same product again
MySQL says
ERROR 1062 (23000): Duplicate entry '1-14' for key 'wishlist.PRIMARY'

A many-to-many relationship, so a junction table, with a composite primary key that makes each customer and product pair unique. The second save of product 14 by customer 1 is refused. In the real shop, the two columns would also be foreign keys to customers and products (Day 3). added_at belongs here because it describes the pair, like quantity in order_items.

4. payments: one payment attempt (an order can have several, Day 10). web_events: one action on the website, such as a page view or an add to cart (Day 13 rolled these up to sessions). reviews: one review, by one customer, of one product, but with exact copies to remove first (Day 11). Saying the grain out loud before writing a query prevents most counting mistakes.

SELECT COUNT(*)                               AS order_lines,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id, product_key)  AS order_product_pairs
FROM fact_sales;
Result

The two counts are equal, so no product appears twice in one order: the grain really is "one product in one order". If they differed, two lines for the same product would need either adding together during the load, or a line number in the key. COUNT(DISTINCT a, b) counts distinct combinations of the two columns.

🎯 Interview corner

Design questions are common for analyst and analytics engineer roles: explaining normal forms, sketching a schema on a whiteboard, and knowing how a warehouse differs from an app database.

Concept questions

Q1What is normalisation, and why do it?

Organising tables so each fact is stored once, with tables linked by keys. It prevents update anomalies (copies that disagree), insert anomalies (can't store one thing without another), and delete anomalies (deleting one thing loses another). It makes writing safe and consistent, at the cost of more joins when reading.

Q2Explain 1NF, 2NF and 3NF with examples.

1NF: one value per cell, with no lists like 'fitness,home,gift' and no repeated columns like phone1, phone2. 2NF: with a composite key, every column depends on the whole key, so product_name doesn't belong in order_items (order_id, product_id). 3NF: no column depends on another non-key column, so state doesn't belong in customers next to city. "The key, the whole key, and nothing but the key."

Q3What's the difference between OLTP and OLAP?

OLTP systems run the business: many small, fast reads and writes (place an order), with normalised tables for safe updates. OLAP systems analyse it: large reads across history, batch loads rather than constant writes, and denormalised designs like star schemas, often in columnar cloud warehouses.

Q4What's a fact table, and what's a dimension table?

A fact table records events at a stated grain, with foreign keys to dimensions and numeric measures to add up (quantity, revenue). Dimension tables hold the descriptive attributes used to filter and group (product name, category, city, date parts). Facts are usually long and narrow. Dimensions are shorter and wider.

Q5What is the grain of a fact table, and why decide it first?

What one row represents, such as one product on one order. It decides which dimensions apply, which measures make sense, and how rows can be added up. Mixing grains, like order totals and line totals in one table, causes double counting. Choose the most detailed grain available, because you can aggregate up but never break totals back down.

Q6Star schema or snowflake schema?

A star keeps each dimension in one denormalised table (category name inside the product dimension). A snowflake normalises dimensions into further tables (product, then category). Stars mean fewer joins and simpler, faster queries, and are the usual choice. Snowflakes save some space and avoid repeated values in big dimensions.

Q7Natural key or surrogate key?

A natural key comes from the real world (email, PAN). A surrogate key is a meaningless generated number. Natural keys change, get mistyped and turn out not to be unique: our shop's emails were all different as typed, but stood for only 3,003 people across 3,028 accounts. Use a surrogate primary key, plus a UNIQUE rule on the cleaned natural value. In warehouses, surrogate keys also make Type 2 history possible.

Q8What is a slowly changing dimension? Explain Type 1 and Type 2.

A dimension whose attributes change occasionally, like a customer's city. Type 1 overwrites the old value: simple, but history is rewritten. Type 2 adds a new row with a new surrogate key and validity dates (valid_from, valid_to, is_current), so past facts keep the old description. Type 3 adds a "previous value" column.

Q9ETL or ELT?

ETL transforms data before loading it into the warehouse. ELT loads raw data first and transforms it inside the warehouse with SQL, using tools like dbt. ELT has become common with powerful cloud warehouses, because the raw data is kept and transformations are just versioned SQL.

Q10When would you denormalise?

When reading matters far more than writing: reports, dashboards, warehouses. And when the copies are maintained by one controlled process (a nightly load) or computed on the fly (a view), so they can't drift apart. Not in the tables the app writes to all day.

Practical questions

P1Design the tables for a library: members borrow books, and a book can be borrowed many times.
A sketch: one good answer
CREATE TABLE members (
    member_id  INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    name       VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
    email      VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL UNIQUE,
    joined_on  DATE NOT NULL
);

CREATE TABLE books (
    book_id    INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    title      VARCHAR(200) NOT NULL,
    author     VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
    isbn       CHAR(13) UNIQUE
);

CREATE TABLE loans (                         -- the junction between members and books
    loan_id     INT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    member_id   INT  NOT NULL,
    book_id     INT  NOT NULL,
    borrowed_on DATE NOT NULL,
    due_on      DATE NOT NULL,
    returned_on DATE,                        -- NULL while the book is still out
    FOREIGN KEY (member_id) REFERENCES members (member_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (book_id)   REFERENCES books (book_id)
);

Members and books are many-to-many over time, so loans sits between them. It gets its own loan_id instead of a composite key, because the same member can borrow the same book more than once. Say that out loud: it shows you thought about the grain ("one loan"). Follow-ups to expect: several copies of one book (add a copies table between books and loans), and authors with several books (an authors table, which 3NF suggests).

P2Tags were stored as a comma-separated list. Count how many products have each tag.
SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE product_tags (product_name VARCHAR(50), tags VARCHAR(200));

INSERT INTO product_tags VALUES
    ('Yoga Mat',       'fitness,home,gift'),
    ('Cotton T-Shirt', 'clothing,outfit,gift'),
    ('Smartwatch',     'fitness,electronics,gift');

SELECT jt.tag, COUNT(*) AS products
FROM product_tags pt,
     JSON_TABLE(CONCAT('["', REPLACE(pt.tags, ',', '","'), '"]'),
                '$[*]' COLUMNS (tag VARCHAR(50) PATH '$')) AS jt
GROUP BY jt.tag
ORDER BY products DESC, jt.tag;
Result
+-------------+----------+
| tag         | products |
+-------------+----------+
| gift        |        3 |
| fitness     |        2 |
| clothing    |        1 |
| electronics |        1 |
| home        |        1 |
| outfit      |        1 |
+-------------+----------+

The trick: REPLACE and CONCAT turn fitness,home,gift into the JSON list ["fitness","home","gift"], and JSON_TABLE (MySQL 8) turns each item into its own row. Then it's an ordinary GROUP BY. It works, but the right answer to give next is: "and then I'd fix the design, with one row per product and tag", because the query only exists to undo a 1NF violation.

P3In a Type 2 customer dimension, find the row that was current on 14 May 2024 for customer 1.
SQL
CREATE TEMPORARY TABLE dim_customer_history (
    customer_key INT PRIMARY KEY, customer_id INT, city VARCHAR(50),
    valid_from DATE, valid_to DATE, is_current TINYINT
);

INSERT INTO dim_customer_history VALUES
    (1, 1, 'Hyderabad', '2023-11-02', '2024-06-30', 0),
    (2, 1, 'Pune',      '2024-07-01', '9999-12-31', 1);

SELECT customer_key, city
FROM dim_customer_history
WHERE customer_id = 1
  AND '2024-05-14' BETWEEN valid_from AND valid_to;
Result
+--------------+-----------+
| customer_key | city      |
+--------------+-----------+
|            1 | Hyderabad |
+--------------+-----------+

The date falls inside exactly one row's period. In a real load, the same condition goes into the join between new sales and the dimension: ON d.customer_id = s.customer_id AND s.order_date BETWEEN d.valid_from AND d.valid_to. A good follow-up point: the periods must never overlap or leave gaps, or a sale could match two rows or none. That's worth a data quality check (Day 11).

Quick recap

  • OLTP databases run the business with many small writes, so they're normalised. OLAP systems analyse it with big reads, so they're designed for easy reading.
  • Relationships are one-to-many (a foreign key on the many side), many-to-many (a junction table like order_items) or one-to-one (rare). Prefer surrogate primary keys, with UNIQUE rules on cleaned natural values.
  • Normalisation prevents update, insert and delete anomalies. 1NF: one value per cell. 2NF: depend on the whole key. 3NF: nothing but the key.
  • Denormalise for reading, safely: as views, or as copies written only by a controlled load.
  • A star schema has a fact table (events at a stated grain, with measures) and dimension tables (descriptions). Choose the most detailed grain. Stars beat snowflakes for simple queries.
  • Every warehouse load ends with a reconciliation: our fact table had the same 9,508 lines and ₹1,31,72,860 as the source.
  • Slowly changing dimensions: Type 1 overwrites, Type 2 adds a dated row with a new surrogate key to keep history true.
  • ETL/ELT moves and shapes data into the warehouse, often with dbt. Columnar cloud warehouses reward selecting only the columns you need.
