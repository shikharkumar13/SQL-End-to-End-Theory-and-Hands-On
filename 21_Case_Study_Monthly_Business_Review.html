The good-to-haves are done. Day 20 ended with a mock interview, and in section 4 you investigated a falling number from scratch. In a real job, nobody should have to discover a 23% drop by accident: there's a monthly business review that shows it, explains it, and is on the manager's desk in the first week of the month. That's today's case study. It's the first of three, and they're different from every earlier day: there are no new SQL features. Instead, you'll see how the pieces from Days 1 to 20 fit together into work someone actually reads.

Before you start. Run USE online_store_full;. You'll use the Day 11 clean layer, Day 12's calendar, Day 13's customer_summary, and from Day 19 the fiscal_year() function, the monthly_sales table and its refresh_monthly_sales() procedure. One check in section 2 also reads Day 17's shop_warehouse. If you've deleted any of these, rerun that day's Build boxes.

1The brief

Ritu Malhotra, the shop's CEO (she's employee 1, at the top of Day 16's org chart), sends a short message:

"Can you send me a one-page review of last month? How did we do, what changed and why, and anything I should worry about. I'd like the same every month from now on."

Before writing a single query, settle three things. Day 15's take-home and Day 20's interviews made the same point: the thinking you do before the SQL decides whether the answer is right.

1. Which month? Our data ends on 31 December 2024. Day 12 showed that December isn't settled: orders from the last week are still Pending or Shipped, and their returns haven't arrived. So the latest month we can review properly is November 2024. In real life, you'd send it in the first days of December, and mark some numbers as provisional (Try this, question 5 shows which).

2. Compared with what? A number on its own means nothing. Every metric gets two comparisons, and they tell different stories (Day 12):

  • Last month (October 2024): is the business moving up or down right now? But October was Diwali, the busiest month of the year, so November will almost always look worse.
  • The same month last year (November 2023): is it growing, with the season taken out? This is usually the fairer test.
  • And for the bigger picture, the year so far, both by calendar year and by financial year (April to March, Day 19's fiscal_year()).

3. What exactly does each word mean? Write the definitions down, and put them at the bottom of the review. When two people get different numbers, this table is the first place to look (Day 20, scenario 2):

2Check the data first

The fastest way to lose a manager's trust is a number that turns out to be wrong. So before any analysis, three quick checks, all things you've done before. First, did the shop record sales every day (Day 12's calendar finds missing days)?

The lesson: a summary table is safe for the measures that add up (revenue), and not for counts of distinct things. So the scorecard counts orders from clean_sales.

3The scorecard

The top of the review is a scorecard: one row per metric, with the month, the comparisons, and the change. Since Ritu wants this every month, save it as a procedure from the start (Day 19), with the month as its input. Here it is, and then a walk through how it works:

Build 1: the scorecard procedure
DROP PROCEDURE IF EXISTS monthly_scorecard;

DELIMITER $$

CREATE PROCEDURE monthly_scorecard(IN p_month DATE)    -- the first day of the month to review
BEGIN
    WITH monthly AS (                                   -- step 1: one row per month
        SELECT DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m-01')                AS month_start,
               SUM(line_total)                                     AS revenue,
               COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)                            AS orders,
               SUM(line_total) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)          AS avg_order_value,
               COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)                         AS buyers,
               100 * SUM(line_total - line_cost) / SUM(line_total) AS gross_margin_pct
        FROM clean_sales
        WHERE order_date >= p_month - INTERVAL 1 YEAR
          AND order_date <  p_month + INTERVAL 1 MONTH
        GROUP BY month_start
    ), new_buyers AS (
        SELECT DATE_FORMAT(first_order_date, '%Y-%m-01') AS month_start,
               COUNT(*)                                   AS new_customers
        FROM customer_summary
        GROUP BY month_start
    ), long_form AS (                                   -- step 2: one row per month and metric
                  SELECT month_start, 1 AS sort_order, 'Revenue' AS metric, revenue AS value FROM monthly
        UNION ALL SELECT month_start, 2, 'Orders',          orders           FROM monthly
        UNION ALL SELECT month_start, 3, 'Avg order value', avg_order_value  FROM monthly
        UNION ALL SELECT month_start, 4, 'Buyers',          buyers           FROM monthly
        UNION ALL SELECT month_start, 5, 'New customers',   new_customers    FROM new_buyers
        UNION ALL SELECT month_start, 6, 'Gross margin %',  gross_margin_pct FROM monthly
    ), wide AS (                                        -- step 3: the three months side by side
        SELECT sort_order, metric,
               MAX(CASE WHEN month_start = p_month                    THEN value END) AS this_month,
               MAX(CASE WHEN month_start = p_month - INTERVAL 1 MONTH THEN value END) AS last_month,
               MAX(CASE WHEN month_start = p_month - INTERVAL 1 YEAR  THEN value END) AS last_year
        FROM long_form
        GROUP BY sort_order, metric
    )
    SELECT metric,                                      -- step 4: format and compare
           FORMAT(this_month, IF(sort_order = 6, 1, 0), 'en_IN') AS this_month,
           FORMAT(last_month, IF(sort_order = 6, 1, 0), 'en_IN') AS last_month,
           FORMAT(last_year,  IF(sort_order = 6, 1, 0), 'en_IN') AS same_month_last_year,
           ROUND(IF(sort_order = 6, this_month - last_month,
                                    100 * (this_month / last_month - 1)), 1) AS vs_last_month,
           ROUND(IF(sort_order = 6, this_month - last_year,
                                    100 * (this_month / last_year - 1)), 1)  AS vs_last_year
    FROM wide
    ORDER BY sort_order;
END$$

DELIMITER ;
SQL

Result

| metric          | this_month | last_month | same_month_last_year | vs_last_month | vs_last_year |
+-----------------+------------+------------+----------------------+---------------+--------------+
| Revenue         | 9,84,300   | 10,69,768  | 7,80,842             |          -8.0 |         26.1 |
| Orders          | 397        | 426        | 315                  |          -6.8 |         26.0 |
| Avg order value | 2,479      | 2,511      | 2,479                |          -1.3 |          0.0 |
| Buyers          | 322        | 325        | 263                  |          -0.9 |         22.4 |
| New customers   | 111        | 100        | 96                   |          11.0 |         15.6 |
| Gross margin %  | 43.3       | 37.7       | 39.5                 |           5.7 |          3.9 |
+-----------------+------------+------------+----------------------+---------------+--------------+

Four steps, each one a pattern you know:

  1. monthly works out every metric for each month from November 2023 to November 2024, grouped with Day 6's DATE_FORMAT and Day 12's half-open range. new_buyers counts first orders from Day 13's customer_summary.
  2. long_form turns the six metric columns into rows, one per month and metric, with UNION ALL (Day 16). This is the opposite of a pivot, often called an unpivot.
  3. wide then pivots the months into columns with MAX(CASE ...), exactly as on Days 8 and 16. Unpivot, then pivot: that's how you turn "a row per month" into "a row per metric".
  4. The last SELECT formats the numbers the Indian way with FORMAT(..., 'en_IN') (9,84,300, like Day 11's ₹1,31,72,860), and works out the changes. The margin is already a percentage, so its change is shown in percentage points (43.34 − 37.69 = 5.65, shown rounded as 5.7). Saying "margin rose 15%" for a move from 37.7% to 43.3% would confuse everyone.

Now read it the way Ritu will. Against October, almost everything is down: revenue −8%, orders −7%. Against last November, almost everything is up: revenue +26%, orders +26%, buyers +22%. Both are true. October was the Diwali month, so the fair reading is the second one: November grew strongly on last year, and dipped after the festive peak, as expected. And two numbers stand out on both comparisons: 111 new customers (the second-best month the shop has had, after October 2023's 114), and a gross margin of 43.3%, up almost 6 points in a month. Sections 4 and 5 explain the big moves.

4Why revenue moved

Revenue is orders × average order value, so any change in revenue comes from more (or fewer) orders, bigger (or smaller) orders, or both. Day 20 did this split by eye. Here's a version that puts a rupee figure on each part:

SQL
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m-01') AS month_start,
           SUM(line_total)          AS revenue,
           COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date >= '2023-11-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-01'
    GROUP BY month_start
), pairs AS (
    SELECT 'vs October 2024' AS comparison,
           b.revenue AS revenue_before, b.orders AS orders_before,
           a.revenue AS revenue_now,    a.orders AS orders_now
    FROM monthly a JOIN monthly b ON a.month_start = '2024-11-01' AND b.month_start = '2024-10-01'
    UNION ALL
    SELECT 'vs November 2023', b.revenue, b.orders, a.revenue, a.orders
    FROM monthly a JOIN monthly b ON a.month_start = '2024-11-01' AND b.month_start = '2023-11-01'
)
SELECT comparison,
       ROUND(revenue_now - revenue_before) AS revenue_change,
       ROUND((orders_now - orders_before) * (revenue_before / orders_before))         AS from_number_of_orders,
       ROUND((revenue_now / orders_now - revenue_before / orders_before) * orders_now) AS from_order_size
FROM pairs;
Result
+------------------+----------------+-----------------------+-----------------+
| comparison       | revenue_change | from_number_of_orders | from_order_size |
+------------------+----------------+-----------------------+-----------------+
| vs October 2024  |         -85468 |                -72825 |          -12643 |
| vs November 2023 |         203458 |                203267 |             191 |
+------------------+----------------+-----------------------+-----------------+

How the split works: first, price the change in orders at the old order value ("if each order had been the same size as before, what would the extra orders be worth?"). Then the change in order value is applied to this month's orders. The two parts always add up exactly to the total change, which is a good check that the arithmetic is right (−72,825 − 12,643 = −85,468).

  • Against October, revenue fell by about ₹85,000, and 85% of that is fewer orders. Orders also got slightly smaller, but that's the smaller part.
  • Against last November, revenue rose by about ₹2 lakh, and all of it is more orders. The average order is the same size as a year ago, to the rupee (₹2,479). The shop isn't selling bigger baskets. It's selling to more people.

That last point is the kind of sentence a CEO remembers. It also tells her where growth comes from, and so where to look if it stops: in the number of orders, which Day 13's funnel and new-customer numbers explain.

5What sold, and the margin

Next, the categories, side by side for the three months, with each category's margin in October and November. It's Day 16's pivot, built straight from clean_sales:

SQL
SELECT category_name,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' THEN line_total END)) AS nov_2024,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-11-01'
                      THEN line_total END))                                  AS oct_2024,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2023-12-01' THEN line_total END)) AS nov_2023,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-11-01'
                            THEN line_total - line_cost END)
                 / SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-11-01'
                            THEN line_total END), 1)                         AS oct_margin,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' THEN line_total - line_cost END)
                 / SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' THEN line_total END), 1) AS nov_margin
FROM clean_sales
WHERE (order_date >= '2023-11-01' AND order_date < '2023-12-01')
   OR (order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-01')
GROUP BY category_name

Why did the margin jump almost 6 points?

Look at the margin columns. Clothing earns over 50% margin, and Electronics under 30%. So when spending moves from Electronics to Clothing, the shop's overall margin rises even if no single product's margin changes. That's a mix effect. But every category's own margin also went up a little, which is a rate effect. How much of the jump is each? Recalculate October's margin twice, changing one thing at a time:

SQL
WITH by_category AS (
    SELECT category_name,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-11-01' THEN line_total END)             AS oct_revenue,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-11-01' THEN line_total - line_cost END) AS oct_profit,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' THEN line_total END)             AS nov_revenue,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' THEN line_total - line_cost END) AS nov_profit
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-01'
    GROUP BY category_name
), shares AS (
    SELECT *,
           oct_revenue / SUM(oct_revenue) OVER () AS oct_share,     -- each category's share of the month
           nov_revenue / SUM(nov_revenue) OVER () AS nov_share
    FROM by_category
)
SELECT ROUND(100 * SUM(oct_profit) / SUM(oct_revenue), 1)        AS october_margin,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(nov_share * oct_profit / oct_revenue), 1) AS november_mix_october_margins,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(oct_share * nov_profit / nov_revenue), 1) AS october_mix_november_margins,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(nov_profit) / SUM(nov_revenue), 1)        AS november_margin
FROM shares;
Result
+----------------+------------------------------+------------------------------+-----------------+
| october_margin | november_mix_october_margins | october_mix_november_margins | november_margin |
+----------------+------------------------------+------------------------------+-----------------+
|           37.7 |                         40.4 |                         40.7 |            43.3 |
+----------------+------------------------------+------------------------------+-----------------+

Read it left to right. October's actual margin was 37.7%. Keep October's margins but switch to November's mix of categories, and it rises to 40.4%: about 2.7 points come from selling more clothing. Keep October's mix but use November's margins, and it rises to 40.7%: about 3 points come from better margins inside the categories. The two together explain the move to 43.3%. (They don't add up perfectly, because mix and rate changed at the same time. That small overlap is normal.)

And why did margins inside the categories improve? Remember Day 15's clue: line_total is what the customer paid, with any coupon discount already taken off, while the cost stays the same. So heavy discounting lowers the margin:

       ROUND(100 * SUM(line_total - line_cost) / SUM(line_total), 1)           AS margin_both_months
FROM clean_sales
WHERE order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-01'

6Customers and channels

Where did the extra orders come from: new customers or returning ones? One row per order first, with a flag for "first order", then group (the same shape as Day 20's investigation):

SQL
WITH orders AS (
    SELECT s.order_id,
           MIN(s.order_date)                        AS order_date,
           MIN(s.channel)                           AS channel,
           SUM(s.line_total)                        AS revenue,
           MIN(s.order_date) = MIN(c.first_order_date) AS is_first_order
    FROM clean_sales s
    JOIN customer_summary c ON c.customer_id = s.customer_id
    WHERE (s.order_date >= '2023-11-01' AND s.order_date < '2023-12-01')
       OR (s.order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND s.order_date < '2024-12-01')
    GROUP BY s.order_id
)
SELECT DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m')                              AS month,
       SUM(is_first_order)                                           AS first_orders,
       SUM(NOT is_first_order)                                       AS repeat_orders,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(CASE WHEN NOT is_first_order THEN revenue END)
                 / SUM(revenue), 1)                                  AS repeat_revenue_pct,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(channel = 'app'), 1)                          AS app_orders_pct
FROM orders
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;
Result

| 2024-10 |          100 |           326 |               78.1 |           48.6 |
| 2024-11 |          111 |           286 |               71.7 |           50.1 |
+---------+--------------+---------------+--------------------+----------------+
  • Against last November, both kinds of order grew: first orders by 15 (96 to 111), and repeat orders by 67 (219 to 286). Most of the growth is customers coming back, the thing Day 13 said the shop most needed.
  • Against October, first orders went up and repeat orders went down by 40. The post-Diwali dip is entirely returning customers taking a break, which is normal after a big shopping month. (That's also why Day 22's question, "who stays and who leaves?", matters so much.)
  • About 7 in 10 rupees still come from repeat orders. And the app now takes half of all orders, against 46% a year ago (Day 16 saw the app overtake the website in 2024).

7Returns and operations

Day 11 left a question open: is "revenue" before or after returns? The review should show both. Every return in our data arrives within 25 days of its order, so by now November's orders have had their full return window. Match each sold line to its return, if any, with a LEFT JOIN (Day 15's returns question):

SQL
SELECT DATE_FORMAT(s.order_date, '%Y-%m')                       AS month,
       ROUND(SUM(s.line_total))                                  AS revenue,
       ROUND(COALESCE(SUM(r.refund_amount), 0))                  AS refunded,
       ROUND(SUM(s.line_total) - COALESCE(SUM(r.refund_amount), 0)) AS net_revenue,
       ROUND(100 * COUNT(r.return_id) / COUNT(*), 1)             AS lines_returned_pct
FROM clean_sales s
LEFT JOIN clean_returns r ON r.order_item_id = s.order_item_id
WHERE (s.order_date >= '2023-11-01' AND s.order_date < '2023-12-01')
   OR (s.order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND s.order_date < '2024-12-01')
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;

Last, the operational health checks: cancellations and failed payments. Notice the trick in this query: CASE without an ELSE gives NULL for rows outside the month, and AVG skips NULLs, so each column averages only its own month. Day 20 showed how that NULL-skipping can mislead. Here, it's exactly what we want:

SQL
SELECT 'Orders cancelled %' AS measure,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-11-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-01'
                            THEN status = 'Cancelled' END), 1) AS nov_2024,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-10-01' AND order_date < '2024-11-01'
                            THEN status = 'Cancelled' END), 1) AS oct_2024,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2023-11-01' AND order_date < '2023-12-01'
                            THEN status = 'Cancelled' END), 1) AS nov_2023
FROM clean_orders
WHERE is_test = 0 AND is_duplicate = 0
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Payment attempts failed %',
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN payment_date >= '2024-11-01' AND payment_date < '2024-12-01'
                            THEN payment_status = 'failed' END), 1),
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN payment_date >= '2024-10-01' AND payment_date < '2024-11-01'
                            THEN payment_status = 'failed' END), 1),
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN payment_date >= '2023-11-01' AND payment_date < '2023-12-01'
                            THEN payment_status = 'failed' END), 1)
FROM clean_payments;

And the website (Day 13's funnel). One trap: Day 12's issue log says the tracking was down on the morning of 19 November, so sessions from that day are left out, exactly as Day 13 did:

WITH sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id,
           MIN(event_time)                    AS started,
           MAX(event_type = 'product_view')   AS viewed,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart')    AS carted,

    FROM clean_web_events

       ROUND(100 * SUM(carted) / SUM(viewed), 1)          AS viewers_who_carted_pct,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(purchased) / SUM(checked_out), 1)  AS checkouts_paid_pct,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(purchased), 1)                     AS conversion_pct
FROM sessions
WHERE started < '2024-12-01'

8Write it up

You now have far more numbers than Ritu wants to read. The skill is choosing. Day 20's advice applies: lead with the answer, round the numbers, separate what the data shows from what you think, and end with actions. Here's the page:

Monthly review: November 2024

Headline. Revenue was ₹9.8 lakh: up 26% on November 2023, and down 8% on October's Diwali peak, as expected after a festive month. All of the growth on last year is more orders. The average order is the same size as a year ago. Calendar year to date: ₹72.9 lakh, up about 60%.

Going well. 111 new customers, our second-best month ever. Repeat orders up 31% on last November. Gross margin back to 43%. Failed payments down to about 3% of attempts.

To watch. The margin's 6-point rise is a recovery, not a record: October (38%) was our lowest month of the year, pulled down by Diwali gadgets and discounts, and November is back in our usual 42 to 44%. Winter clothing helped (the Winter Jacket sold nine times October's amount). Clothing returns: 1 line in 8 comes back, three-quarters of all refunds, mostly about size. Returning customers took a break after Diwali: 40 fewer repeat orders than in October.

Suggested actions. Add a size guide to the clothing pages. Plan a gentle win-back message for October's festive buyers who haven't returned (Day 13's segments can pick them).

Notes. Revenue is completed orders before returns (₹9.25 lakh after returns). Website figures leave out 19 November (tracking outage). Definitions are listed below this page.

Notice what's not there: no SQL, no tables with ten columns, no number with more than three significant figures. Every number in it, though, is one you can point to in a query above. If Ritu asks "why?", you have the answer one level down.

🎯 Interview corner

Reporting case questions test structure and judgment: which numbers, compared with what, and how you explain a change. These come up in analyst interviews more often than any single SQL feature.

Concept questions

Q1How would you structure a monthly business review?

Check the data first. Then a scorecard of a few agreed metrics, each against last month and the same month last year, with the year to date. Then explanations of the biggest moves, broken into their parts (orders and order value, mix and rate, new and returning). Then risks and suggested actions. Then notes: definitions, data issues, and anything provisional.

Q2Month-on-month or year-on-year: which is better?

They answer different questions. Month-on-month shows direction right now, but seasonality (a festive peak, a short February) can dominate it. Year-on-year compares like with like, so it's usually the fairer measure of growth. Show both, and explain any difference between them.

Q3Revenue went up. How do you explain why?

Break it into parts that multiply to revenue: orders × average order value, or customers × orders per customer × order value. Put a rupee figure on each part by changing one at a time. Then split the biggest part by segment (new and returning, channel, category) until the change sits in a few places.

Q4What's a mix effect?

A change in an overall rate caused by a change in the make-up of the total, rather than in any of the parts. If high-margin clothing becomes a bigger share of sales, the overall margin rises even if no category's margin changes. To separate mix from rate, recalculate holding one of them fixed.

Q5Why reconcile numbers before publishing?

Because the same number often lives in several places (views, a warehouse, summary tables), and they can disagree through filters, timing or grain. Checking that they match catches mistakes before a manager does. Reconciling also tells you which measures a summary table can safely give: sums yes, distinct counts usually no.

Q6How do you handle a month that isn't complete yet?

Either wait until it settles, or report it with the incomplete parts clearly marked as provisional (open orders, returns still arriving) and update them later. Never compare an unfinished period directly with a finished one without saying so.

Practical questions

P1Revenue is flat month on month, but orders are up 10%. What happened, and what do you check?

Average order value fell by about 9%, since revenue = orders × order value. Check whether the mix shifted towards cheaper categories or products, whether discounts or coupons increased, whether a new low-priced product launched, and whether new customers (who often buy less) made up a bigger share of orders.

P2Turn a table with one row per month and columns for revenue, orders and buyers into one row per metric, with a column per month.

Unpivot, then pivot: UNION ALL one SELECT per metric to get (month, metric, value) rows, then GROUP BY metric with MAX(CASE WHEN month = ... THEN value END) for each month. That's the scorecard in section 3.

P3Gross margin went from 37.7% to 43.3%. How do you report the change?

As "up about 5.7 percentage points", not "up 15%" (which is the relative change, 5.7 ÷ 37.7). Then explain it: how much came from the mix of categories, and how much from margins within them. And look at the longer trend before celebrating: in section 5, October turned out to be the unusual month.

Quick recap

  • Before any SQL: pick a month that has settled, decide the comparisons (last month, last year, year to date), and write down a definition for every metric.
  • Check the data first: no missing days, no open orders, and the same totals in every place that stores them. Summary tables are safe for sums, not for distinct counts.
  • A scorecard procedure (unpivot with UNION ALL, then pivot with MAX(CASE ...)) turns next month's review into one CALL. Show margin changes in percentage points.
  • Explain revenue as orders × order value, with a rupee figure for each part. November grew 26% on last year, all from more orders.
  • Explain margin as mix and rate. November's rise was about half the shift to clothing and half better margins within categories. And check the longer trend: it was a recovery from an unusually low October, not a record.
  • Show revenue after returns too, and check the operational basics: cancellations, failed payments, the website funnel, with known data gaps left out and stated.
  • The written review leads with the answer, rounds the numbers, separates facts from suggestions, and ends with actions and notes.
