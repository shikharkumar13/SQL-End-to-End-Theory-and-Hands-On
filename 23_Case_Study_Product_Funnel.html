1The brief

Ritu is planning 2025 with two of her team: Sanjay Kulkarni, Head of Operations, who decides what stock to buy, and Ananya Krishnan, Head of Technology, whose team runs the website. Her note:

"Before we plan next year: which products really carry us, and are any causing trouble? Sanjay needs that for buying. Was 2024's growth real demand, or just our price rises? Ananya wants to add 'frequently bought together' to product pages: is there anything to show? And where does the website lose people?"

Five questions, each with its own section. Before any SQL, settle the periods, using rules from earlier days:

  • Sales: 1 January to 24 December 2024, the last settled day (Day 12). When comparing with 2023, use the same days of 2023, 1 January to 24 December, so neither year gets an extra week (Day 21's "compare like with like").
  • Returns: orders placed before 30 November 2024. Returns arrive within 25 days (Day 21), so later orders haven't had their full window.
  • Website: 1 October to 24 December 2024, the only months with tracking, leaving out 19 November, when tracking was down (Day 12's issue log, Day 13).

2Check the data first

A product analysis assumes every product had the same chance to sell. Did it? Some products were launched recently, some were withdrawn, and the products table only stores a launch date and an is_active flag, not the day a product stopped. The sales themselves tell you the rest:

SQL
SELECT p.product_id, p.product_name,
       COALESCE(c.category_name, '(none)')  AS category,
       p.launch_date, p.is_active,
       DATE(MIN(s.order_date))              AS first_sale,
       DATE(MAX(s.order_date))              AS last_sale
FROM products p
LEFT JOIN categories c  ON c.category_id = p.category_id
LEFT JOIN clean_sales s ON s.product_id  = p.product_id
WHERE p.launch_date >= '2023-01-01'
   OR p.is_active = 0
   OR p.category_id IS NULL
GROUP BY p.product_id, p.product_name, c.category_name, p.launch_date, p.is_active
ORDER BY p.launch_date;

Now the base for sections 3 and 5: one row per product for 2024, including its days on sale. Active products count to 24 December; withdrawn ones to their last sale:

Build 1: each product's 2024, with days on sale
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW product_2024 AS
SELECT s.product_id, s.product_name, s.category_name,
       SUM(s.quantity)                  AS units,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.order_id)       AS orders,
       SUM(s.line_total)                AS revenue,
       SUM(s.line_total - s.line_cost)  AS profit,
       DATEDIFF(IF(p.is_active = 1, '2024-12-24', DATE(MAX(s.order_date))),
                GREATEST(p.launch_date, '2024-01-01')) + 1 AS days_on_sale
FROM clean_sales s
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = s.product_id
WHERE s.order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND s.order_date < '2024-12-25'
GROUP BY s.product_id, s.product_name, s.category_name, p.is_active, p.launch_date;

GREATEST picks the later of two dates, so a product launched before 2024 starts on 1 January, and one launched in May starts in May. The + 1 counts both the first and the last day.

3Which products matter?

Sanjay's classic tool is an ABC analysis: sort products by profit, keep a running total, and split them into class A (the products that make the first 80% of profit), B (the next 15%) and C (the last 5%). That's a common split; some businesses use 70/20/10 instead. A products get the most stock and attention; C products are the ones to question. The running total is Day 9's window frame:

SQL
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT product_name, category_name, revenue, profit, days_on_sale,
           100 * SUM(profit) OVER (ORDER BY profit DESC
                                   ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
               / SUM(profit) OVER ()                                     AS running_pct
    FROM product_2024
),
classed AS (
    SELECT *,
           CASE WHEN running_pct - 100 * profit / SUM(profit) OVER () < 80 THEN 'A'
                WHEN running_pct - 100 * profit / SUM(profit) OVER () < 95 THEN 'B'
                ELSE 'C' END AS abc_class
    FROM ranked
)
SELECT abc_class,
       COUNT(*)                                             AS products,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(profit) / SUM(SUM(profit)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_profit,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(revenue) / SUM(SUM(revenue)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_revenue
FROM classed
GROUP BY abc_class
ORDER BY abc_class;
Result
+-----------+----------+---------------+----------------+
| abc_class | products | pct_of_profit | pct_of_revenue |
+-----------+----------+---------------+----------------+
| A         |       33 |          80.2 |           78.7 |
| B         |       16 |          15.5 |           16.8 |
| C         |       11 |           4.3 |            4.4 |
+-----------+----------+---------------+----------------+

One detail in classed: a product joins class A if the running total before it is still under 80%, which is why its own share is subtracted. Otherwise the product that crosses the 80% line would be pushed into B.

You may have heard of the 80/20 rule: 20% of products make 80% of the profit. Not here. It takes over half the range, 33 products, to reach 80%. With 60 fairly similar products, profit is spread widely. That's worth knowing before anyone suggests cutting the range hard: there's no small core carrying everything. The rule is a pattern seen in many businesses, not a law, so always check it.

4The problem product

A product can sell well and still cost the shop money, through returns and unhappy customers. Compare each product's return rate with its own category's (clothing is always returned more, Day 15), and add its rating from verified buyers. Day 22's give-or-take shows which gaps are bigger than luck:

SQL
WITH sold AS (
    SELECT s.product_id, s.product_name, s.category_name,
           COUNT(*)           AS lines_sold,
           COUNT(r.return_id) AS returned
    FROM clean_sales s
    LEFT JOIN clean_returns r ON r.order_item_id = s.order_item_id
    WHERE s.order_date < '2024-11-30'              -- full 25-day return window
    GROUP BY s.product_id, s.product_name, s.category_name
),
with_category AS (
    SELECT *,
           returned / lines_sold                                       AS rate,
           SUM(returned) OVER w / SUM(lines_sold) OVER w               AS category_rate
    FROM sold
    WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY category_name)
),
ratings AS (
    SELECT r.product_id, COUNT(*) AS reviews, AVG(r.rating) AS avg_rating
    FROM clean_reviews r
    JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = r.customer_id
    WHERE r.rating IS NOT NULL
      AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM clean_sales s
                  WHERE s.customer_id = c.master_customer_id
                    AND s.product_id  = r.product_id)       -- verified buyers only
    GROUP BY r.product_id
)
SELECT w.product_name, w.category_name, w.lines_sold,
       ROUND(100 * w.rate, 1)                                            AS returned_pct,
       CAST(200 * SQRT(w.rate * (1 - w.rate) / w.lines_sold) AS DECIMAL(4,1)) AS give_or_take,
       ROUND(100 * w.category_rate, 1)                                   AS category_pct,
       r.reviews,
       ROUND(r.avg_rating, 2)                                            AS avg_rating
FROM with_category w
LEFT JOIN ratings r ON r.product_id = w.product_id
ORDER BY w.rate / w.category_rate DESC
LIMIT 6;
Result

| product_name             | category_name  | lines_sold | returned_pct | give_or_take | category_pct | reviews | avg_rating |

| USB-C Hub                | Electronics    |        185 |         15.7 |          5.3 |          5.1 |      35 |       2.77 |
| Data Science Handbook    | Books          |        100 |          4.0 |          3.9 |          1.4 |      22 |       3.27 |
| The Pragmatic Programmer | Books          |         63 |          3.2 |          4.4 |          1.4 |      13 |       3.85 |

One product stands out on every measure: the USB-C Hub. About 1 in 6 hubs comes back, against about 1 in 20 for electronics as a whole, and even the low end of its range (15.7 − 5.3 = 10.4%) is twice the category's rate. Its verified buyers rate it 2.77 out of 5, across 35 reviews. Compare the next rows: the Data Science Handbook also looks high next to its category, but it rests on four returns out of 100. That's below Day 22's minimum of about 10 for the give-or-take to work, so it's simply too few to tell. One more detail works in the hub's favour: the category's 5.1% includes the hub's own returns, so the rest of electronics comes back even less often than that. Why do the hubs come back?

5Real growth, or just higher prices?

Revenue grew strongly in 2024, but prices went up too. How much? Since discounts only ever lower a price, the highest price a product sold at in a year is its list price that year, as long as it sold at least once without a discount (true for every product here, with dozens of sales a year) and the list price didn't change mid-year:

SQL
WITH list_prices AS (
    SELECT product_id,
           MAX(CASE WHEN YEAR(order_date) = 2023 THEN unit_price END) AS price_2023,
           MAX(CASE WHEN YEAR(order_date) = 2024 THEN unit_price END) AS price_2024
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY product_id
)
SELECT SUM(price_2024 > price_2023)                                    AS prices_raised,
       SUM(price_2024 = price_2023)                                    AS prices_unchanged,
       SUM(price_2024 < price_2023)                                    AS prices_cut,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(CASE WHEN price_2024 > price_2023
                            THEN price_2024 / price_2023 - 1 END), 1)  AS avg_rise_pct
FROM list_prices;
Result
+---------------+------------------+------------+--------------+
| prices_raised | prices_unchanged | prices_cut | avg_rise_pct |
+---------------+------------------+------------+--------------+
|            24 |               32 |          0 |          9.0 |
+---------------+------------------+------------+--------------+

24 products went up in price, by 9% on average, and the rest stayed the same. (Five products only sold in one of the two years, so they're in none of the columns.) So some growth is price. To measure it, split the change in revenue into parts, like Day 21's orders and order value, but with more pieces. For products sold in both years:

  • Volume: more units sold, valued at 2023's average price per unit.
  • Mix: buying a different mix of products. If customers move to pricier products, revenue rises even at the same prices.
  • Price: paying more for the same products. This is the price actually paid, so it includes both list price rises and any change in discounts.

Plus products that were new in 2024, and products no longer sold. Same days in both years:

SQL
WITH by_product AS (
    SELECT product_id,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-01-01' THEN quantity END)   AS units_23,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-01-01' THEN line_total END) AS rev_23,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-01-01' THEN quantity END)   AS units_24,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-01-01' THEN line_total END) AS rev_24
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE (order_date >= '2023-01-01' AND order_date < '2023-12-25')
       OR (order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND order_date < '2024-12-25')
    GROUP BY product_id
),
continuing AS (                                    -- sold in both years
    SELECT SUM(units_23) AS u23, SUM(rev_23) AS r23,
           SUM(units_24) AS u24, SUM(rev_24) AS r24,
           SUM(units_24 * rev_23 / units_23) AS r24_at_2023_prices
    FROM by_product
    WHERE units_23 IS NOT NULL AND units_24 IS NOT NULL
)
          SELECT '1. More units (volume)'       AS part, ROUND((u24 - u23) * r23 / u23)          AS rupees FROM continuing
UNION ALL SELECT '2. Pricier products (mix)',           ROUND(r24_at_2023_prices - u24 * r23 / u23)       FROM continuing
UNION ALL SELECT '3. Higher prices paid (price)',       ROUND(r24 - r24_at_2023_prices)                   FROM continuing
UNION ALL SELECT '4. New products',                     ROUND(SUM(rev_24))   FROM by_product WHERE units_23 IS NULL
UNION ALL SELECT '5. Products no longer sold',          -ROUND(SUM(rev_23))  FROM by_product WHERE units_24 IS NULL
UNION ALL SELECT 'Total change',                        ROUND(SUM(COALESCE(rev_24, 0)) - SUM(COALESCE(rev_23, 0))) FROM by_product;
Result
+-------------------------------+---------+
| part                          | rupees  |
+-------------------------------+---------+
| 1. More units (volume)        | 1958945 |
| 2. Pricier products (mix)     |   29714 |
| 3. Higher prices paid (price) |  256311 |
| 4. New products               |  688802 |
| 5. Products no longer sold    |  -69765 |
| Total change                  | 2864008 |
+-------------------------------+---------+

The key line is units_24 * rev_23 / units_23: each product's 2024 units, priced at what that product sold for in 2023. Comparing it with 2024's units at the overall 2023 average price (u24 * r23 / u23) gives the mix; comparing it with what 2024 really brought in gives the price. As always with a bridge, the parts add up to the total change (to within a rupee of rounding), which checks the arithmetic.

The answer for Ritu: of the ₹28.6 lakh of growth, about ₹19.6 lakh (two-thirds) is more units, real demand. New products, mostly the Earbuds and the Dumbbell Set, added ₹6.9 lakh. Higher prices added ₹2.6 lakh, under a tenth. The mix barely moved. So the growth is real: people bought more, not just paid more.

6What sells together?

Ananya's "frequently bought together" idea needs pairs of products in the same order. That's a self join (Day 7): join the order lines to themselves on order_id. The condition a.product_id < b.product_id keeps each pair once (not both A–B and B–A) and stops a product pairing with itself. (Careful with names: LINES is a reserved word in MySQL, so the CTE is called basket.)

SQL
WITH basket AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT order_id, product_id, product_name
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date < '2024-12-25'
),
total AS (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders FROM basket),
per_product AS (
    SELECT product_id, COUNT(*) AS orders_with
    FROM basket
    GROUP BY product_id
),
pairs AS (
    SELECT a.product_id AS id_a, b.product_id AS id_b,
           a.product_name AS product_a, b.product_name AS product_b,
           COUNT(*) AS together
    FROM basket a
    JOIN basket b ON b.order_id = a.order_id
                 AND a.product_id < b.product_id
    GROUP BY id_a, id_b, product_a, product_b
)
SELECT product_a, product_b, together,
       ROUND(pa.orders_with * pb.orders_with / t.orders, 1)      AS expected,
       ROUND(together * t.orders / (pa.orders_with * pb.orders_with), 2) AS lift
FROM pairs
JOIN per_product pa ON pa.product_id = id_a
JOIN per_product pb ON pb.product_id = id_b
CROSS JOIN total t
ORDER BY together DESC
LIMIT 8;
Result

| product_a           | product_b          | together | expected | lift |

| Cotton T-Shirt      | Phone Case         |       21 |     27.4 | 0.77 |

The most common pair is the Cotton T-Shirt with the Yoga Mat: 25 orders. Should the shop show yoga mats on the T-shirt page? Look at expected first. The T-shirt is the shop's most popular product, and if people chose items completely independently, the two would land in the same order about 24 times anyway, just by being popular. The measure that adjusts for this is lift:

  • expected = (orders with A × orders with B) ÷ all orders: how often the pair would meet by chance.
  • lift = together ÷ expected. Around 1 means "no connection". Above 1 means they're bought together more than chance; below 1, less.

Every top pair has a lift close to 1 (or below). They're just popular products meeting by chance. A widget built on raw counts would recommend the T-shirt with everything. So sort by lift instead, but only for pairs bought together at least 10 times, because a lift built on one or two orders means nothing:

SQL
WITH basket AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT order_id, product_id, product_name
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date < '2024-12-25'
),
total AS (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders FROM basket),
per_product AS (
    SELECT product_id, COUNT(*) AS orders_with
    FROM basket
    GROUP BY product_id
),
pairs AS (
    SELECT a.product_id AS id_a, b.product_id AS id_b,
           a.product_name AS product_a, b.product_name AS product_b,
           COUNT(*) AS together
    FROM basket a
    JOIN basket b ON b.order_id = a.order_id
                 AND a.product_id < b.product_id
    GROUP BY id_a, id_b, product_a, product_b
)
SELECT product_a, product_b, together,
       ROUND(pa.orders_with * pb.orders_with / t.orders, 1)      AS expected,
       ROUND(together * t.orders / (pa.orders_with * pb.orders_with), 2) AS lift
FROM pairs
JOIN per_product pa ON pa.product_id = id_a
JOIN per_product pb ON pb.product_id = id_b
CROSS JOIN total t
WHERE together >= 10
ORDER BY lift DESC
LIMIT 5;
Result
+------------------------------+---------------------+----------+----------+------+
| product_a                    | product_b           | together | expected | lift |
+------------------------------+---------------------+----------+----------+------+
| Mechanical Keyboard          | USB-C Hub           |       11 |      6.4 | 1.71 |
| Cotton T-Shirt               | Cycling Gloves      |       10 |      6.8 | 1.46 |
| Cotton T-Shirt               | Table Lamp          |       10 |      7.1 | 1.41 |
| Stainless Steel Water Bottle | Power Bank 20000mAh |       14 |     10.3 | 1.36 |
| Power Bank 20000mAh          | Atomic Habits       |       13 |     10.2 | 1.27 |

Now the top pair has a story: a Mechanical Keyboard with a USB-C Hub, two things people plug into a laptop. 11 orders against 6.4 expected, lift 1.7. Is it real? Be careful. The shop's orders contain about 1,500 different pairs, and when you look at that many, a few will reach a lift like this by luck alone (Day 22's warning about checking many groups at once). And 11 orders is a small number. Honest answer for Ananya:

  • In this data, baskets look mostly like independent choices. A "frequently bought together" widget would have little real pattern to show yet.
  • The keyboard and hub pairing is plausible and cheap to test: show the hub on the keyboard's page to a random half of visitors, and compare the two halves (an A/B test, the same idea as Day 22's holdout). A before-and-after comparison is weaker, because the season changes in between.
  • But not until the hub's quality problem (section 4) is fixed. Recommending a product that 1 in 6 people send back would just mean more returns and more unhappy customers.

7The website funnel

Day 13 built the funnel for the whole site, and Day 21 checked it month by month. Ananya's question is where to work, so split it by device. Each column is the share who reached a step out of those who reached the step before, so you can see exactly where people drop out:

SQL
WITH sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id,
           MIN(event_time)                    AS started,
           MIN(device)                        AS device,
           MAX(event_type = 'product_view')   AS viewed,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart')    AS carted,
           MAX(event_type = 'checkout_start') AS checked_out,
           MAX(event_type = 'purchase')       AS purchased
    FROM clean_web_events
    GROUP BY session_id
    HAVING started < '2024-12-25'
       AND NOT (started >= '2024-11-19' AND started < '2024-11-20')    -- tracking outage
)
SELECT device,
       COUNT(*)                                         AS sessions,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(viewed), 1)                      AS viewed_pct,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(carted) / SUM(viewed), 1)        AS carted_of_viewers,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(checked_out) / SUM(carted), 1)   AS checkout_of_carts,
       ROUND(100 * SUM(purchased) / SUM(checked_out), 1) AS paid_of_checkouts,
       ROUND(100 * AVG(purchased), 1)                   AS conversion_pct,
       SUM(purchased)                                   AS purchases
FROM sessions
GROUP BY device
ORDER BY sessions DESC;

Mobile is where the shop lives: about 7 in 10 sessions. And mobile converts worst of the big two: 5.4% of mobile sessions end in a purchase, against 8.4% on desktop. Fewer mobile visitors look at a product at all (63% against 73%), far fewer of those who look add to cart (24.5% against 35.3%), and fewer carts reach checkout (55% against 64%). One step goes the other way: mobile's paid_of_checkouts is the highest. That's a quirk of the tracking, not good news. Every app purchase is recorded with the device "mobile" (join the purchase events to clean_orders on order_id and compare channel with device to see it), so "mobile" mixes app shoppers with mobile-website shoppers. For sessions that didn't buy, there's no order to tell them apart. Worth asking Ananya's team to record the app as its own device. (Tablets convert worst of all, but on under 1,000 sessions; fix mobile first.)

What would a fix be worth? A rough what-if: if mobile viewers added to cart at desktop's rate, and every later step stayed the same, mobile purchases would rise by about 35.3 ÷ 24.5 ≈ 1.44 times: about 330 more orders over these twelve weeks. Treat that as an upper limit, not a forecast. "Mobile" includes the app, and desktop shoppers may simply be more determined buyers (people often browse on phones and buy on laptops), so some of the gap would never close. The way to know is an A/B test: Day 22's holdout idea, applied to a new design.

Last, the products themselves. Which ones turn views into sales, and which are looked at but rarely bought? clean_web_events records a product_id on views and add-to-carts, so count the sessions that looked at each product, and those that added it to the cart in a session that ended in a purchase. (The purchase event itself has no product_id, so this is as close as the tracking gets to "bought it".)

SQL
WITH product_sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id, product_id,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart') AS carted
    FROM clean_web_events
    WHERE product_id IS NOT NULL
      AND event_time >= '2024-10-01' AND event_time < '2024-12-25'
      AND NOT (event_time >= '2024-11-19' AND event_time < '2024-11-20')
    GROUP BY session_id, product_id
),
buying_sessions AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT session_id FROM clean_web_events WHERE event_type = 'purchase'
),
per_product AS (
    SELECT ps.product_id,
           COUNT(*)                                       AS sessions_viewing,
           SUM(ps.carted AND b.session_id IS NOT NULL)    AS bought
    FROM product_sessions ps
    LEFT JOIN buying_sessions b ON b.session_id = ps.session_id
    GROUP BY ps.product_id
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT p.product_name, pp.sessions_viewing, pp.bought,
           100 * pp.bought / pp.sessions_viewing                          AS rate,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY pp.bought / pp.sessions_viewing DESC) AS best,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY pp.bought / pp.sessions_viewing)      AS worst
    FROM per_product pp
    JOIN products p ON p.product_id = pp.product_id
)
SELECT product_name, sessions_viewing, bought,
       ROUND(rate, 1) AS bought_per_100_views
FROM ranked
WHERE best <= 5 OR worst <= 5
ORDER BY rate DESC;

🎯 Interview corner

Product and funnel questions are common for analyst roles in e-commerce, retail and any company with an app. They test whether you look past the obvious number.

Concept questions

Q1What is an ABC analysis, and what can go wrong with it?

Sort products by a value (profit is usually better than revenue), keep a running share, and class them A (first ~80%), B (next ~15%) and C (the rest). It goes wrong when products didn't have the same chance to sell: new launches and withdrawn products land in C unfairly. Compare per day on sale, and check the class boundaries with the share before each product.

Q2Explain support, confidence and lift.

For a pair A and B: support is the share of all orders containing both. Confidence (A → B) is the share of orders with A that also contain B. Lift is support divided by what chance predicts, P(A) × P(B), or equivalently together ÷ expected. Lift near 1 means no connection. Popular products have high support and confidence with everything, which is why lift matters.

Q3Revenue grew 56%. How do you tell how much was price?

A price-volume-mix bridge. For products sold in both periods, value this period's units at last period's prices. The difference between that and last period's revenue splits into volume (more units) and mix (pricier products). The difference between actual revenue and the repriced figure is price. New and discontinued products are separate lines. The parts must add up to the total change.

Q4Why show funnel steps as a share of the step before, rather than of all sessions?

Because it shows where people leave. Overall conversion tells you that mobile is worse; step rates tell you which step is worse, which is what a team can actually fix. Always show both.

Q5Mobile converts at 5% and desktop at 8%. If mobile matched desktop, how many extra orders would that be?

You can work out a what-if (mobile sessions × the gap), but call it an upper limit. The users differ, not just the device: many people browse on phones and buy on laptops. Some of the gap can never close. Only an A/B test of a mobile change tells you what's really gained.

Practical questions

P1Write SQL to find pairs of products bought in the same order.

Self join the order lines: FROM order_lines a JOIN order_lines b ON b.order_id = a.order_id AND a.product_id < b.product_id, then GROUP BY the two products and COUNT(*). The < keeps each pair once and excludes a product paired with itself. Use DISTINCT order lines first if a product can appear twice in one order. (And in MySQL, don't name the table or CTE lines: it's a reserved word.)

P2A product has lots of page views but few sales. What do you check?

Whether it was in stock and its price compared with similar products; whether it's seasonal; its reviews and return rate; and the step where people drop (viewed but never added to cart points to price, photos or description; added but not bought points to delivery cost or checkout). Compare with similar products over the same days.

P3Our best-selling product has the highest return rate. Is that bad?

Compare the return rate with its own category's, not the shop's, since some categories always return more (clothing for size). Check whether the gap is bigger than chance for its sales volume, then look at return reasons: "faulty" points to quality, "not as described" to the product page, "wrong size" to sizing information.

Quick recap

  • Check which products could have sold all period. Launches, withdrawals and missing categories need allowing for: compare per day on sale, over the same days.
  • Use ratings from verified buyers only. Match reviewers on the master customer ID.
  • ABC classes come from a running total. Rank by profit, not revenue, and don't assume 80/20: here 33 of 60 products make 80% of the profit.
  • Judge a product's return rate against its own category, with a give-or-take. The USB-C Hub came back about three times as often as its category, mostly faulty.
  • A price-volume-mix bridge separates real demand from price rises. 2024's growth was mostly more units.
  • For "bought together", raw counts just find popular products. Use lift, demand a minimum number of orders, and remember that some pairs look special by luck when you check hundreds.
  • Split the funnel by device and step. Mobile loses shoppers at browsing and add-to-cart. Treat what-if gains as upper limits, and test changes properly.
