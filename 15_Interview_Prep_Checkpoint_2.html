1What SQL interviews look like

"SQL interview" covers several different formats. Most hiring processes for analyst roles use two or three of them:

FormatWhat happensWhat helps most
Online testTimed questions on a website: multiple choice, plus short queries checked automaticallySpeed with the basics (Days 5 to 9), and reading the question carefully
Live codingYou write queries in a shared editor while an interviewer watches, usually for 30 to 60 minutesThinking out loud (section 2), and staying calm when something errors (Day 14)
Take-homeA dataset and a list of questions, with a few days to answer them and write up what you foundClean, commented SQL and a clear write-up (section 6)
Case discussion"How would you measure whether the new checkout is working?" Talked through, sometimes with SQL on a whiteboardThe analysis patterns (Day 13), and asking good questions
Concept questionsQuick questions: "What's the difference between WHERE and HAVING?"The Interview corners from every day, and the rapid-fire list at the end of this article

Whatever the format, interviewers are looking at the same five things:

  1. Is the answer right? Including the details: completed orders only, each customer counted once.
  2. Did you handle the edge cases? NULLs, ties, duplicates, groups with no rows, unfinished periods.
  3. Is the SQL clear? Could a colleague read it and change it next month?
  4. Can you explain it? Why this join, why this function, what you assumed.
  5. Does the number make sense? Did you check it against something you know?

Notice that only the first one is about the final answer. Interviewers often say they learn more from how you get there than from the query itself. That's good news: the method is something you can practise.

2Think out loud: a five-step method

Think of a doctor with a new patient. A good doctor doesn't hear "my stomach hurts" and write a prescription. They ask questions first, examine, then decide, and they tell you what they're thinking as they go. A good SQL answer works the same way. Here's the method, on a real question:

The question: "Which customers spent more with us in 2024 than in 2023?"

Step 1: clarify the question out loud

Almost every interview question is a little vague on purpose, to see if you notice. Say what you'd want to know, and if there's no one to ask, say what you'll assume:

  • "By 'spent', do you mean completed orders only?" Assume yes, which is clean_sales.
  • "What about customers who only bought in one of the two years?" Someone who first bought in 2024 trivially spent more than in 2023. Assume we only compare customers who bought in both years, and report the others separately.
  • "Should a person with two accounts count once?" Yes. clean_sales already uses the master ID (Day 11).

Step 2: name the grain of the answer

"The answer has one row per customer, with their 2023 and 2024 spending side by side." Saying the grain out loud (Day 10) tells you what to GROUP BY before you write anything.

Step 3: plan in small steps

Write the plan as names first, then fill them in. Interviewers love seeing this, because it shows structure:

The plan, before any real SQL
-- yearly:  one row per customer: spent_2023, spent_2024
-- then:    keep customers with spending in both years
-- then:    count how many spent more in 2024, and list the biggest increases

Step 4: build each step, and look at it

Start with the counts. They tell you the shape of the data before you trust any list:

SQL
WITH yearly AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS spent_2023,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS spent_2024
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*)                                            AS customers,
       SUM(spent_2023 > 0 AND spent_2024 > 0)              AS bought_both_years,
       SUM(spent_2023 > 0 AND spent_2024 > spent_2023)     AS spent_more_in_2024,
       SUM(spent_2023 = 0)                                 AS only_2024,
       SUM(spent_2024 = 0)                                 AS only_2023
FROM yearly;
Result
+-----------+-------------------+--------------------+-----------+-----------+
| customers | bought_both_years | spent_more_in_2024 | only_2024 | only_2023 |
+-----------+-------------------+--------------------+-----------+-----------+
|      2115 |               345 |                211 |      1026 |       744 |
+-----------+-------------------+--------------------+-----------+-----------+

Say what you see: "2,115 customers in total. 345 bought in both years, and 211 of them spent more in 2024. The three groups add up: 345 + 1,026 + 744 = 2,115, so nobody was lost." That last sentence is a check, and saying it out loud scores points. Now the list:

SQL
WITH yearly AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS spent_2023,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS spent_2024
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT y.customer_id,
       c.first_name,
       c.last_name,
       ROUND(y.spent_2023)                AS spent_2023,
       ROUND(y.spent_2024)                AS spent_2024,
       ROUND(y.spent_2024 - y.spent_2023) AS increase
FROM yearly y
JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = y.customer_id
WHERE y.spent_2023 > 0
  AND y.spent_2024 > y.spent_2023
ORDER BY increase DESC
LIMIT 5;
Result
+-------------+------------+-------------+------------+------------+----------+
| customer_id | first_name | last_name   | spent_2023 | spent_2024 | increase |
+-------------+------------+-------------+------------+------------+----------+
|        1800 | Pranav     | Chopra      |       6061 |      77314 |    71253 |
|        1035 | Ankit      | Bose        |       1659 |      49501 |    47842 |
|        1379 | Anika      | Patel       |       2122 |      44651 |    42529 |
|        2221 | Hiroshi    | Wilson      |        218 |      34151 |    33933 |
|         918 | Olivia     | Al-Mansoori |       2820 |      35956 |    33136 |
+-------------+------------+-------------+------------+------------+----------+

Step 5: test the edge cases, then explain

Before saying "done", run through the usual suspects, out loud:

  • NULLs: "I used ELSE 0 inside SUM, so a customer with no 2023 orders gets 0, not NULL. That's why spent_2023 = 0 works as a test."
  • Duplicates: "clean_sales has one row per order line, but I'm summing money, not counting orders, so lines are exactly right."
  • Dates: "The data starts in 2023, so 'before 2024' really means 2023. If there were older data, I'd add a start date."
  • Ties: "Two customers with the same increase would be ordered arbitrarily. If the cut-off at 5 mattered, I'd add a tie-breaker (Day 9)."

Then the answer, in one sentence a manager would understand: "Of the 345 customers who bought in both years, 211 (61%) spent more in 2024. The biggest increase was Pranav Chopra, up from about ₹6,000 to ₹77,000."

When you get stuck. It happens to everyone. Say what you're trying to do: "I need the previous row's value here. That's LAG, I just need a moment for the syntax." Interviewers care far more about the right idea than perfect recall. Also ask which database they use. LIMIT versus TOP (Day 14) is a fair question, not a weakness.

3The classic question types

Interview questions look endless, but most of them are one of about a dozen types in disguise. Learn to recognise the type from the words in the question, and you already know the pattern. Every one of these has appeared in this series:

TypeIt sounds likeThe patternThe trapDay
Nth highest, top N"second highest", "top 3 in each"DENSE_RANK or ROW_NUMBER in a CTE, then filterTies: which ranking function did they mean?5, 8, 9
Latest or first per group"each customer's most recent order"ROW_NUMBER() ... = 1No tie-breaker, so a different answer each run9
Things that never happened"customers who never ordered"LEFT JOIN ... IS NULL, or NOT EXISTSNOT IN with a NULL returns nothing5, 7, 8
Duplicates"find the duplicates", "keep one of each"GROUP BY ... HAVING COUNT(*) > 1, ROW_NUMBERDeciding what "the same" means6, 10, 11
Rows compared within a table"earns more than their manager", "bought together"Self joinCounting each pair twice7
Group filters"customers with more than 5 orders"HAVINGPutting the aggregate in WHERE (error 1111)6
Rates and shares"cancellation rate", "% of total"SUM(CASE ...), SUM(...) OVER ()The wrong denominator, or integer division6, 8, 9, 14
Change over time"month over month", "vs last year"LAG on a gap-free seriesMissing months, unfinished periods9, 12
Running totals"cumulative", "year to date"SUM() OVER (ORDER BY ...)The default frame lumps ties together9, 12
Pivot"one column per month"SUM(CASE WHEN ...)Columns must be known in advance8, 14
Repeat and retention"how many came back", "cohort"First order per customer, then compareNot giving everyone the same time13

Notice the trap column. Interviewers choose questions because of the trap. "Second highest salary" isn't hard to write. It's there to see whether you think about ties, and about what happens when there isn't a second one. When you recognise the type, say the trap out loud before they ask: "There might be ties for first place, so I'll use DENSE_RANK."

4Practice: people and teams

The employees table is a favourite interview playground: 30 staff with a department, a salary, and a manager_id that points at another employee (a self-referencing foreign key, from Day 3). It came in clean, so we use it directly. Try each question before opening the answer.

1. Second-highest salary in each department

Show the answer
SQL
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT department,
           first_name,
           last_name,
           job_title,
           salary,
           DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC) AS salary_rank
    FROM employees
)
SELECT department, first_name, last_name, job_title, salary
FROM ranked
WHERE salary_rank = 2
ORDER BY department;
Result
+------------------+------------+-----------+--------------------------+------------+
| department       | first_name | last_name | job_title                | salary     |
+------------------+------------+-----------+--------------------------+------------+
| Customer Support | Aditi      | Joshi     | Support Team Lead        | 1100000.00 |
| Finance          | Gaurav     | Jain      | Accountant               |  780000.00 |
| Marketing        | Dev        | Sandhu    | Growth Analyst           | 1900000.00 |
| Operations       | Kiran      | Rao       | Warehouse Lead           | 1400000.00 |
| Technology       | Varun      | Chopra    | Senior Software Engineer | 3050000.00 |
+------------------+------------+-----------+--------------------------+------------+

The type is "Nth highest per group". DENSE_RANK because "second-highest salary" means the second-highest value: if two people shared the top salary, the next salary down should still count as second (Day 9). Then the edge case, said out loud: Management is missing. It has only one person, the CEO, so there is no second salary. Is that what the interviewer wants? If they'd rather see Management with a blank, start from a list of departments and LEFT JOIN this result to it, the same idea as Day 12's grid.

2. Who earns more than their manager?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT e.first_name || '' AS employee,
       e.job_title,
       e.salary,
       m.first_name AS manager,
       m.job_title  AS manager_title,
       m.salary     AS manager_salary
FROM employees e
JOIN employees m ON m.employee_id = e.manager_id
WHERE e.salary > m.salary;
Result
+----------+--------------------------+------------+---------+----------------------------+----------------+
| employee | job_title                | salary     | manager | manager_title              | manager_salary |
+----------+--------------------------+------------+---------+----------------------------+----------------+
|        0 | Senior Software Engineer | 3050000.00 | Meera   | Engineering Manager        |     2900000.00 |
|        0 | Growth Analyst           | 1900000.00 | Neha    | Performance Marketing Lead |     1800000.00 |
+----------+--------------------------+------------+---------+----------------------------+----------------+

Wait: look at the employee column. Those 0s are Day 14's dialect trap, which slipped into this answer on purpose: in MySQL, || means OR, not "join text". The correct version uses CONCAT:

SQL
SELECT CONCAT(e.first_name, ' ', e.last_name) AS employee,
       e.job_title,
       e.salary,
       CONCAT(m.first_name, ' ', m.last_name) AS manager,
       m.job_title AS manager_title,
       m.salary    AS manager_salary
FROM employees e
JOIN employees m ON m.employee_id = e.manager_id
WHERE e.salary > m.salary;
Result
+--------------+--------------------------+------------+-------------+----------------------------+----------------+
| employee     | job_title                | salary     | manager     | manager_title              | manager_salary |
+--------------+--------------------------+------------+-------------+----------------------------+----------------+
| Varun Chopra | Senior Software Engineer | 3050000.00 | Meera Menon | Engineering Manager        |     2900000.00 |
| Dev Sandhu   | Growth Analyst           | 1900000.00 | Neha Kapoor | Performance Marketing Lead |     1800000.00 |
+--------------+--------------------------+------------+-------------+----------------------------+----------------+

The self join (Day 7): the same table twice, as e for the employee and m for their manager. An inner join is right here, because the CEO has no manager and can't earn more than one. Two people out-earn their managers: a senior engineer and a growth analyst. That's common in real companies, where an expert can be paid more than the person who manages them. A good answer says so, rather than treating it as a data error. (And the broken first version is a reminder to look at every result before you call it finished.)

3. Who is paid above their department's average?

Show the answer
SQL
WITH with_average AS (
    SELECT department,
           CONCAT(first_name, ' ', last_name)          AS employee,
           salary,
           AVG(salary) OVER (PARTITION BY department) AS dept_average
    FROM employees
)
SELECT department,
       employee,
       salary,
       ROUND(dept_average)          AS dept_average,
       ROUND(salary - dept_average) AS above_by
FROM with_average
WHERE salary > dept_average
ORDER BY department, above_by DESC;
Result

| department       | employee        | salary     | dept_average | above_by |

| Marketing        | Farah Siddiqui  | 3000000.00 |      1590000 |  1410000 |
| Marketing        | Dev Sandhu      | 1900000.00 |      1590000 |   310000 |
| Marketing        | Neha Kapoor     | 1800000.00 |      1590000 |   210000 |

A window AVG (Day 9) puts the department's average on every row, so each person can be compared with it. The filter has to be in the outer query, because WHERE runs before window functions. Notice the averages: one high salary pulls the department average up, so in most departments only the senior people are above it.

4. Which managers have the most direct reports?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT m.employee_id,
       CONCAT(m.first_name, ' ', m.last_name) AS manager,
       m.job_title,
       COUNT(*) AS direct_reports
FROM employees m
JOIN employees e ON e.manager_id = m.employee_id
GROUP BY m.employee_id, manager, m.job_title
ORDER BY direct_reports DESC, m.employee_id
LIMIT 5;
Result
+-------------+-----------------+-------------------------+----------------+
| employee_id | manager         | job_title               | direct_reports |
+-------------+-----------------+-------------------------+----------------+
|           1 | Ritu Malhotra   | Chief Executive Officer |              5 |
|          14 | Aditi Joshi     | Support Team Lead       |              4 |
|          20 | Meera Menon     | Engineering Manager     |              3 |
|          24 | Priya Sharma    | Data Analytics Lead     |              3 |
|           2 | Sanjay Kulkarni | Head of Operations      |              2 |
+-------------+-----------------+-------------------------+----------------+

Self join, then GROUP BY the manager side. A common follow-up is "and who manages nobody?" That's the "never happened" type: WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM employees e WHERE e.manager_id = m.employee_id), which finds 19 of the 30. And a harder follow-up, "list everyone under the Head of Technology, at every level", needs a recursive CTE. That's Day 16.

5Practice: sales

Four more, on the clean sales data. These are closer to what a business would really ask.

1. The top 2 products by revenue in each category, for 2024

Show the answer
SQL
WITH product_revenue AS (
    SELECT category_name,
           product_name,
           SUM(line_total) AS revenue
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND order_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY category_name, product_name
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT category_name,
           product_name,
           revenue,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY category_name
                              ORDER BY revenue DESC, product_name) AS rn
    FROM product_revenue
)
SELECT category_name, product_name, ROUND(revenue) AS revenue
FROM ranked
WHERE rn <= 2
ORDER BY category_name, rn;
Result

| category_name  | product_name                 | revenue |
+----------------+------------------------------+---------+
| Books          | Python for Data Analysis     |  104057 |
| Books          | Atomic Habits                |   89132 |
| Clothing       | Cotton Saree                 |  255585 |
| Clothing       | Kurta Set                    |  249719 |
| Electronics    | Noise-Cancelling Headphones  |  523915 |

2. Which pairs of products are most often bought in the same order?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT a.product_name AS product_1,
       b.product_name AS product_2,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.order_id) AS orders_together
FROM clean_sales a
JOIN clean_sales b
  ON b.order_id   = a.order_id
 AND a.product_id < b.product_id
GROUP BY a.product_id, b.product_id, a.product_name, b.product_name
ORDER BY orders_together DESC, a.product_id, b.product_id
LIMIT 5;
Result
+----------------+--------------------+-----------------+
| product_1      | product_2          | orders_together |
+----------------+--------------------+-----------------+
| Cotton T-Shirt | Yoga Mat           |              25 |
| Cotton T-Shirt | Phone Case         |              21 |
| Cotton T-Shirt | Polo T-Shirt       |              21 |
| Wireless Mouse | Cotton T-Shirt     |              20 |
| Cotton T-Shirt | Ceramic Coffee Mug |              20 |
+----------------+--------------------+-----------------+

A self join on the order: every line paired with every other line in the same order. The key detail is a.product_id < b.product_id. With <>, each pair would appear twice (T-shirt + mat, and mat + T-shirt), and with no condition at all, every product would pair with itself. This is called market basket analysis, and it powers "customers who bought this also bought..." on shopping sites.

And a point that impresses interviewers: the Cotton T-Shirt is in almost every top pair. Is it really "bought together" with everything? More likely, it's just the shop's most popular cheap item, so it turns up in lots of orders by chance. Real basket analysis compares each pair's count with what you'd expect by chance (a measure called lift). Mentioning that a raw count can mislead is the kind of judgement interviewers are looking for.

3. Revenue by category, 2023 vs 2024, with growth

Show the answer
SQL
WITH by_year AS (
    SELECT category_name,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date <  '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS rev_2023,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date >= '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS rev_2024
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY category_name
)
SELECT category_name,
       ROUND(rev_2023) AS rev_2023,
       ROUND(rev_2024) AS rev_2024,
       ROUND((rev_2024 - rev_2023) * 100 / NULLIF(rev_2023, 0), 1) AS growth_pct
FROM by_year
ORDER BY growth_pct DESC;
Result
+----------------+----------+----------+------------+
| category_name  | rev_2023 | rev_2024 | growth_pct |
+----------------+----------+----------+------------+
| Sports         |   624728 |  1608672 |      157.5 |
| Electronics    |  1684332 |  2609135 |       54.9 |
| Clothing       |  1410341 |  2002168 |       42.0 |
| Home & Kitchen |   875289 |  1085177 |       24.0 |
| Books          |   595738 |   648791 |        8.9 |
| Uncategorised  |        0 |    28491 |       NULL |
+----------------+----------+----------+------------+

Conditional aggregation puts the two years side by side (Day 8), then a CTE keeps the growth formula readable. NULLIF(rev_2023, 0) protects against dividing by zero: "Uncategorised" had no 2023 sales, so its growth is NULL ("can't be calculated") instead of an error or a misleading number. The finding: Sports more than doubled, while Books barely grew. That's worth a follow-up question: which Sports products drove it?

4. How many customers bought in every quarter of 2024?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers_every_quarter
FROM (
    SELECT customer_id
    FROM clean_sales
    WHERE order_date >= '2024-01-01' AND order_date < '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY customer_id
    HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT QUARTER(order_date)) = 4
) AS loyal;
Result
+-------------------------+
| customers_every_quarter |
+-------------------------+
|                      75 |
+-------------------------+

"In every X" questions are a group filter in disguise: count the distinct quarters each customer bought in, and keep those with all 4. DISTINCT matters. Without it, a customer with four orders all in March would pass. This "count distinct = total possible" trick also answers "customers who bought from every category" or "products sold in every month".

6Checkpoint 2: the take-home

This is what a real take-home looks like. A hiring manager sends you access to the shop's database and six questions, with a weekend to answer them. Treat it as a test: give yourself about two hours, answer all six before opening any answer, and write one sentence of finding for each. If you can do most of them, you've reached Checkpoint 2.

The brief: "We're planning next year. Using our 2023 and 2024 data, please answer the six questions below. For each, give us the SQL, the result, and what you think it means for the business."

1. How much of each year's revenue came from new customers, and how much from returning ones?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT YEAR(s.order_date) AS yr,
       CASE WHEN YEAR(s.order_date) = YEAR(c.first_order_date)
            THEN 'New this year' ELSE 'Returning' END AS customer_type,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) AS customers,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.order_id)    AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(s.line_total))      AS revenue
FROM clean_sales s
JOIN customer_summary c ON c.customer_id = s.customer_id
GROUP BY yr, customer_type
ORDER BY yr, customer_type;
Result
+------+---------------+-----------+--------+---------+
| yr   | customer_type | customers | orders | revenue |
+------+---------------+-----------+--------+---------+
| 2023 | New this year |      1089 |   2343 | 5190428 |
| 2024 | New this year |      1026 |   1894 | 4550628 |
| 2024 | Returning     |       345 |   1425 | 3431805 |
+------+---------------+-----------+--------+---------+

"New" means the customer's first order was in that year, using first_order_date from Day 13's customer_summary. 2023 has no returning customers because our data starts there. That's a limit of the data, not a fact about the business, and it's worth saying. Finding: in 2024, the 345 returning customers brought in ₹34.3 lakh, about 43% of revenue, from a quarter of the buyers. Keeping customers matters as much as winning new ones.

2. Which categories have the highest return rate?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT s.category_name,
       COUNT(*)                                   AS lines_sold,
       COUNT(r.return_id)                         AS lines_returned,
       ROUND(COUNT(r.return_id) * 100 / COUNT(*), 1) AS return_pct,
       ROUND(COALESCE(SUM(r.refund_amount), 0))   AS refunded
FROM clean_sales s
LEFT JOIN clean_returns r ON r.order_item_id = s.order_item_id
GROUP BY s.category_name
ORDER BY return_pct DESC;
Result
+----------------+------------+----------------+------------+----------+
| category_name  | lines_sold | lines_returned | return_pct | refunded |
+----------------+------------+----------------+------------+----------+
| Clothing       |       2505 |            315 |       12.6 |   413629 |
| Electronics    |       2131 |            106 |        5.0 |   209066 |
| Sports         |       1832 |             67 |        3.7 |    76027 |
| Home & Kitchen |       1668 |             48 |        2.9 |    58931 |
| Books          |       1363 |             20 |        1.5 |    18361 |
| Uncategorised  |          9 |              0 |        0.0 |        0 |
+----------------+------------+----------------+------------+----------+

A LEFT JOIN, so lines that were never returned still count in the denominator, and COUNT(r.return_id) counts only the matches (Day 7). COALESCE turns "no refunds" into 0 for Uncategorised (Day 8). Each return points at one order line, and a quick check before writing this showed every return matches a completed line, so nothing is lost. Finding: one in eight clothing lines comes back, more than twice the rate of any other category. Day 11 found that size problems are the most common reason for returns. Better size guides could save a lot of the ₹4.1 lakh refunded on clothing.

3. Which cities have the highest revenue per customer? (Cities with at least 50 customers.)

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT cc.city,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) AS customers,
       ROUND(SUM(s.line_total))      AS revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(s.line_total) / COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id)) AS revenue_per_customer
FROM clean_sales s
JOIN clean_customers cc ON cc.customer_id = s.customer_id
GROUP BY cc.city
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) >= 50
ORDER BY revenue_per_customer DESC
LIMIT 5;
Result
+-----------+-----------+---------+----------------------+
| city      | customers | revenue | revenue_per_customer |
+-----------+-----------+---------+----------------------+
| Jaipur    |        75 |  621651 |                 8289 |
| Gurugram  |        61 |  466022 |                 7640 |
| NULL      |       100 |  762720 |                 7627 |
| Hyderabad |       148 | 1085888 |                 7337 |
| Chennai   |       130 |  944071 |                 7262 |
+-----------+-----------+---------+----------------------+

Look at third place: NULL. The customers with no city formed their own group (GROUP BY puts all NULLs together), and it's big enough to pass the filter. Showing NULL as a "city" in a report to management would be embarrassing. Decide, and say: either exclude them with WHERE cc.city IS NOT NULL, or label them COALESCE(cc.city, 'Not given'). Here, excluding them is right, because the question is about cities. Catching this before handing it in is exactly what take-homes test. Finding: Jaipur customers spend the most each (about ₹8,300), ahead of Gurugram and Hyderabad. The HAVING threshold keeps tiny cities, where one big spender could top the list, out of the ranking.

4. Who processed the most returns, and who is their manager?

Show the answer
SQL
SELECT CONCAT(e.first_name, ' ', e.last_name) AS employee,
       e.job_title,
       m.first_name                           AS manager,
       COUNT(*)                               AS returns_processed,
       ROUND(SUM(r.refund_amount))            AS refunded
FROM clean_returns r
JOIN employees e      ON e.employee_id = r.processed_by
LEFT JOIN employees m ON m.employee_id = e.manager_id
GROUP BY e.employee_id, employee, e.job_title, m.first_name
ORDER BY returns_processed DESC;
Result
+---------------------+--------------------+---------+-------------------+----------+
| employee            | job_title          | manager | returns_processed | refunded |
+---------------------+--------------------+---------+-------------------+----------+
| Pooja Iyer          | Support Agent      | Aditi   |               120 |   181725 |
| Sneha Patil         | Support Agent      | Aditi   |               115 |   143300 |
| Rahul Verma         | Support Agent      | Aditi   |               113 |   159685 |
| Imran Qureshi       | Support Agent      | Aditi   |               112 |   151922 |
| Karthik Subramanian | Returns Specialist | Vikram  |                96 |   139382 |
+---------------------+--------------------+---------+-------------------+----------+

Three tables, one of them twice: returns to the employee who processed them, and the employee to their manager (a self join). The manager join is a LEFT JOIN on purpose: if the CEO ever processed a return, she has no manager, and an inner join would drop her. Finding: five people handle all the returns. Aditi's four support agents share the work almost equally, while Karthik, the returns specialist, handles a little less. Good to know when planning staff for the festive season, when returns peak.

5. Do orders with a coupon spend more or less?

Show the answer
SQL
WITH order_totals AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           MAX(coupon_code) AS coupon_code,
           SUM(line_total)  AS order_total
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT COALESCE(coupon_code, 'No coupon') AS coupon,
       COUNT(*)                           AS orders,
       ROUND(AVG(order_total))            AS avg_order_value,
       ROUND(SUM(order_total) * 100 / SUM(SUM(order_total)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_revenue
FROM order_totals
GROUP BY coupon
ORDER BY orders DESC;
Result
+-----------+--------+-----------------+----------------+
| coupon    | orders | avg_order_value | pct_of_revenue |
+-----------+--------+-----------------+----------------+
| No coupon |   4989 |            2362 |           89.5 |
| WELCOME10 |    498 |            2145 |            8.1 |
| FESTIVE15 |    152 |            1870 |            2.2 |
| NEWYEAR10 |     23 |            1599 |            0.3 |
+-----------+--------+-----------------+----------------+

First roll lines up to one row per order (the grain is orders, so average order value is per order, not per line), then group. MAX(coupon_code) is a small trick: every line of an order has the same coupon, so MAX just picks it up. Every coupon's orders are smaller than orders without one. But don't stop there: is that a fair comparison? A quick check shows that all 498 WELCOME10 orders are customers' first orders, so compare like with like, first orders only:

SQL
WITH order_totals AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           customer_id,
           MIN(order_date)  AS order_date,
           MAX(coupon_code) AS coupon_code,
           SUM(line_total)  AS order_total
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY order_id, customer_id
),
numbered AS (
    SELECT order_total,
           coupon_code,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY order_date, order_id) AS order_number
    FROM order_totals
)
SELECT CASE WHEN coupon_code IS NULL THEN 'No coupon' ELSE 'Coupon' END AS first_order_type,
       COUNT(*)                AS first_orders,
       ROUND(AVG(order_total)) AS avg_order_value
FROM numbered
WHERE order_number = 1
GROUP BY first_order_type;
Result
+------------------+--------------+-----------------+
| first_order_type | first_orders | avg_order_value |
+------------------+--------------+-----------------+
| Coupon           |          538 |            2107 |
| No coupon        |         1577 |            2339 |
+------------------+--------------+-----------------+

Even among first orders, coupon orders are smaller, by about 10%: almost exactly the size of the welcome discount. That suggests a simple explanation: line_total is what the customer paid, with the discount already taken off. If so, coupon shoppers put about the same in their baskets, and just pay less for them. Before writing that in a report, confirm with the team how unit_price is recorded. Finding: "Coupons don't appear to make people buy more. Coupon orders are about 10% smaller, which matches the discount itself. If the goal of the welcome coupon is bigger first orders, it isn't achieving that. Whether it wins customers who wouldn't otherwise buy needs a test." Careful wording about cause and effect is one of the things take-homes are really testing.

6. How many valuable customers have gone quiet?

"Valuable" means they spent at least ₹10,000 in 2023. "Gone quiet" means no completed order since 1 July 2024.

Show the answer
SQL
WITH per_customer AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           SUM(CASE WHEN order_date < '2024-01-01' THEN line_total ELSE 0 END) AS spent_2023,
           MAX(order_date)                                                     AS last_order_date
    FROM clean_sales
    GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*)                AS quiet_valuable_customers,
       ROUND(SUM(spent_2023))  AS their_2023_spend
FROM per_customer
WHERE spent_2023 >= 10000
  AND last_order_date < '2024-07-01';
Result
+--------------------------+------------------+
| quiet_valuable_customers | their_2023_spend |
+--------------------------+------------------+
|                       81 |          1412755 |
+--------------------------+------------------+

Turn the business words into exact rules first, then write the SQL. The brief did that for you here, and in a real take-home you'd write your definitions down yourself. Finding: 81 customers who spent ₹14.1 lakh in 2023 haven't bought for at least six months. That's the win-back list from Day 13's "At risk" segment, sized in money. A campaign that brought back even a quarter of them would be worth a lot.

How to hand in a take-home

  • Lead with the findings. One page: the six answers in plain sentences, numbers included. The SQL goes after it, not before.
  • Write down your assumptions. "Revenue = completed orders, excluding test and duplicate orders. Customers with two accounts counted once." (Day 11's decisions, in one paragraph.)
  • Mention the data problems you found and how you handled them. The NULL city, the unfinished December. It shows care, not weakness.
  • Make every query runnable, in order, with a header comment (Day 14, section 8).
  • End with one or two next steps: "I'd test whether the welcome coupon changes basket size", "I'd look at which Sports products drove the growth".

🎯 Interview corner: the rapid-fire round

Concept questions from Days 1 to 14, the kind that come quickly at the start of an interview. Answer each in a sentence or two before opening it. If one feels shaky, the day number tells you where to go back.

Databases and tables (Days 1 to 4)

Q1What's the difference between a primary key and a foreign key?

A primary key uniquely identifies each row in its own table, and can't be NULL or repeated. A foreign key is a column that points at a primary key in another table (or the same one), and the database refuses values that don't exist there. (Day 3)

Q2What's the difference between DELETE, TRUNCATE and DROP?

DELETE removes rows (all, or those matching WHERE) and can be rolled back inside a transaction. TRUNCATE empties the whole table quickly and resets AUTO_INCREMENT. DROP removes the table itself, structure and all. (Days 3 and 4)

Q3What is a transaction, and what does ACID mean?

A group of statements that succeed or fail together (START TRANSACTION ... COMMIT or ROLLBACK). ACID: Atomic (all or nothing), Consistent (rules are never broken), Isolated (transactions don't see each other's half-done work), Durable (once committed, it survives a crash). (Day 4)

Q4Why use DECIMAL rather than FLOAT for money?

FLOAT stores approximations, so 0.1 + 0.2 isn't exactly 0.3, and small errors add up over millions of rows. DECIMAL stores exact values. (Day 2)

Querying (Days 5 to 9)

Q5In what order does MySQL run the parts of a query?

FROM and joins, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, window functions, SELECT, DISTINCT, ORDER BY, LIMIT. That's why WHERE can't use an aggregate or a window function, and why ORDER BY can use an alias. (Days 6 and 9)

Q6WHERE vs HAVING?

WHERE filters rows before grouping. HAVING filters groups after grouping, so it can use aggregates like COUNT(*) > 5. (Day 6)

Q7COUNT(*) vs COUNT(column) vs COUNT(DISTINCT column)?

COUNT(*) counts rows. COUNT(column) counts non-NULL values in that column. COUNT(DISTINCT column) counts different non-NULL values. After a LEFT JOIN, COUNT(*) gives 1 for an unmatched row, while COUNT(right_table.id) correctly gives 0. (Days 6 and 7)

Q8Explain the join types.

INNER JOIN keeps only matches. LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the left table, with NULLs where there's no match. RIGHT JOIN is the mirror image. FULL OUTER JOIN keeps unmatched rows from both sides (not in MySQL: use a UNION of left and right joins). CROSS JOIN pairs every row with every row. A self join joins a table to itself. (Day 7)

Q9Why can a condition on the right table turn a LEFT JOIN into an inner join?

Unmatched rows have NULL in every right-table column, and a WHERE condition like o.status = 'Completed' is never true for NULL, so those rows are thrown away. Put the condition in the ON clause instead, or filter in a subquery first. (Day 7)

Q10UNION vs UNION ALL?

UNION removes duplicate rows, which costs time. UNION ALL keeps everything. Use UNION ALL unless you specifically need duplicates removed. (Day 7)

Q11Subquery, CTE or view: when would you use each?

A subquery for a small, one-off step inside a query. A CTE to break a longer query into named, readable steps. A view to save a query in the database so many queries and people can reuse it, like Day 11's clean layer. (Days 8 and 9)

Q12ROW_NUMBER vs RANK vs DENSE_RANK?

For values 100, 90, 90, 80: ROW_NUMBER gives 1, 2, 3, 4 (always unique). RANK gives 1, 2, 2, 4 (ties share, then a gap). DENSE_RANK gives 1, 2, 2, 3 (ties share, no gap). (Day 9)

Real-world data (Days 10 to 14)

Q13You're given a database you've never seen. What do you do first?

List the tables and their sizes, work out each table's grain and how they link, look at sample rows, profile the key columns (NULLs, distinct values, ranges), and write an issues log before answering anything. (Day 10)

Q14How would you clean messy data without risking it?

Never change the raw tables. Write each fix as SQL in views (or a cleaned copy), record every business decision, compare counts before and after, and add data quality checks that should return 0. (Day 11)

Q15Why can BETWEEN give the wrong answer for dates, and why might the latest month look like a crash?

On a DATETIME, BETWEEN ... AND '2024-12-31' stops at midnight at the start of the 31st. Use >= the start and < the day after the end. The latest month often looks weak because it's unfinished, or recent orders haven't completed yet, so compare like-for-like. (Day 12)

Q16What is a cohort, and why do recent cohorts often look worse?

A group of customers who started at the same time, followed over time. Recent cohorts have had less time to come back, so compare fixed windows (such as 90 days) for customers who have all had that long, and show "not yet known" as NULL, not 0. (Day 13)

Q17What is SQL injection, and how do you prevent it?

Outside input pasted into SQL text can change the query itself (' OR '1'='1). Prevent it with parameters, such as :status with the value passed separately, never by building SQL from strings. (Day 14)

Q18Name two differences between MySQL and PostgreSQL that can change an answer silently.

Integer division (7/2 is 3.5 in MySQL, 3 in PostgreSQL), and text comparison (MySQL ignores capitals by default, PostgreSQL doesn't). Others: || joins text in PostgreSQL but means OR in MySQL, CONCAT with a NULL, and NULL sort order. (Day 14)

Quick recap

  • SQL interviews come as online tests, live coding, take-homes, case discussions and concept questions. All of them judge correctness, edge cases, clarity, explanation and common sense.
  • The five-step method: clarify the question out loud, name the grain, plan in named steps, build and look at each step, then test edge cases and explain in one plain sentence.
  • Most questions are one of about a dozen types: Nth highest, latest per group, never happened, duplicates, self joins, group filters, rates, change over time, running totals, pivots, retention, funnels. Each has a standard pattern, and a trap the interviewer is waiting to see you handle.
  • Always look at your result before calling it done: a column of 0s, a NULL "city" or a missing department are clues, not details.
  • In a take-home, lead with findings, write down assumptions and data problems, keep every query runnable, and be careful with cause and effect.
  • Checkpoint 2: you have the must-have SQL toolkit for an analyst job. Days 16 to 20 add the good-to-haves, and Days 21 to 23 put everything together in case studies.
