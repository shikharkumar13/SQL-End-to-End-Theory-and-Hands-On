1From queries to patterns

Think of a health checkup. The doctor doesn't invent new tests for each patient. They check the same things every time: blood pressure, pulse, a blood test. Each test answers one question, and together they give a picture of how the patient is doing. The five patterns below are the standard checkup for an online business:

One row per customer

Notice that most of these questions are about customers. But clean_sales has one row per order line. Day 10 called this the grain of a table: what one row means. When the question's grain and the table's grain don't match, the first step is always to build a table at the right grain. So let's make one row per customer, with the facts every pattern needs:

Build 1: customer_summary
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW customer_summary AS
SELECT customer_id,
       MIN(order_date)              AS first_order_date,
       MAX(order_date)              AS last_order_date,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)     AS orders,
       SUM(line_total)              AS revenue,
       SUM(line_total - line_cost)  AS gross_profit
FROM clean_sales
GROUP BY customer_id;

Because it's built on clean_sales, all of Day 11's decisions come along for free: only completed orders, no test or double-submitted orders, and customer_id is the master ID, so a person with two accounts is one customer. gross_profit is what the shop keeps after paying for the products (price minus cost price). Whenever you build a new summary, check it adds up to a number you already trust:

SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                   AS customers,
       SUM(orders)                AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(revenue))        AS revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(gross_profit))   AS gross_profit
FROM customer_summary;
Result
+-----------+--------+----------+--------------+
| customers | orders | revenue  | gross_profit |
+-----------+--------+----------+--------------+
|      2115 |   5662 | 13172860 |      5411119 |
+-----------+--------+----------+--------------+

₹1,31,72,860: exactly Day 11's clean revenue. Nothing was lost or counted twice. The shop keeps about 41% of its revenue as gross profit.

The thread running through today: has everyone had the same chance? On Day 12, December 2024 looked weak because it wasn't finished. Customers have the same problem. Someone who first bought on 20 December 2024 has had 11 days to come back. Someone who first bought in January 2023 has had two years. Compare them directly and new customers will always look worse. You'll meet this trap in three of today's patterns, and the fix is always the same idea: give everyone the same amount of time.

2Repeat purchase: do customers come back?

Winning a new customer costs money: ads, discounts, free delivery. A customer who comes back costs far less. That's why "do they come back?" is usually the first question. Start with how many orders each customer has placed:

SQL
SELECT CASE WHEN orders >= 5 THEN '5 or more' ELSE orders END AS orders_placed,
       COUNT(*)                                        AS customers,
       ROUND(COUNT(*) * 100 / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_customers
FROM customer_summary
GROUP BY orders_placed
ORDER BY orders_placed;
Result
+---------------+-----------+------------------+
| orders_placed | customers | pct_of_customers |
+---------------+-----------+------------------+
| 1             |      1296 |             61.3 |
| 2             |       202 |              9.6 |
| 3             |       183 |              8.7 |
| 4             |        99 |              4.7 |
| 5 or more     |       335 |             15.8 |
+---------------+-----------+------------------+

61% of customers bought once and never again. That's normal for online shops, but it's the biggest opportunity in the data: every one-time buyer is someone who already trusted the shop once. At the other end, 335 customers have placed 5 or more orders.

The single headline number is the repeat rate: the share of customers with two or more orders.

SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                         AS customers,
       SUM(orders >= 2)                 AS repeat_customers,
       ROUND(AVG(orders >= 2) * 100, 1) AS repeat_rate_pct
FROM customer_summary;
Result
+-----------+------------------+-----------------+
| customers | repeat_customers | repeat_rate_pct |
+-----------+------------------+-----------------+
|      2115 |              819 |            38.7 |
+-----------+------------------+-----------------+

orders >= 2 is 1 or 0 for each customer, so SUM counts the repeaters and AVG gives their share: Day 6's trick.

How quickly do they come back?

Day 7 promised this: repeat purchase speed. For every order after a customer's first, how many days since their previous one? LAG (Day 9) puts the previous order's date on the same row:

SQL
WITH orders_only AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, order_id, order_date
    FROM clean_sales
),
gaps AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           DATEDIFF(order_date,
                    LAG(order_date) OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id
                                          ORDER BY order_date, order_id)) AS days_since_previous
    FROM orders_only
)
SELECT CASE
           WHEN days_since_previous <= 30  THEN '1. Within 30 days'
           WHEN days_since_previous <= 60  THEN '2. 31-60 days'
           WHEN days_since_previous <= 90  THEN '3. 61-90 days'
           WHEN days_since_previous <= 180 THEN '4. 91-180 days'
           ELSE '5. Over 180 days'
       END AS gap,
       COUNT(*) AS repeat_orders,
       ROUND(COUNT(*) * 100 / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (), 1) AS pct
FROM gaps
WHERE days_since_previous IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY gap
ORDER BY gap;
Result
+-------------------+---------------+------+
| gap               | repeat_orders | pct  |
+-------------------+---------------+------+
| 1. Within 30 days |          1724 | 48.6 |
| 2. 31-60 days     |           832 | 23.5 |
| 3. 61-90 days     |           392 | 11.1 |
| 4. 91-180 days    |           440 | 12.4 |
| 5. Over 180 days  |           159 |  4.5 |
+-------------------+---------------+------+

Three steps you've seen before: DISTINCT turns order lines into orders, LAG finds each customer's previous order, and each customer's first order has no previous one, so its gap is NULL and gets filtered out. Almost half of all repeat orders came within 30 days of the one before, and 83% within 90 days. In other words, customers who come back tend to come back fast. If someone hasn't returned after three months, the chance that they will is getting small. That's useful for marketing: a "we miss you" email at 60 days is likely to work better than one at 300.

A fair repeat rate

Here's the trap from section 1. The 38.7% includes customers who only discovered the shop last week. They haven't had time to come back yet, so they drag the rate down, and the more new customers the shop wins, the worse its repeat rate looks. The fix: count only repeats within a fixed window, say 90 days after the first order, and only for customers who have had at least 90 days. Our data ends on 31 December 2024, so that's customers whose first order was before 2 October 2024:

SQL
WITH orders_only AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, order_id, order_date
    FROM clean_sales
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers,
       SUM(EXISTS (SELECT 1
                   FROM orders_only o
                   WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id
                     AND o.order_date  > c.first_order_date
                     AND o.order_date  < c.first_order_date + INTERVAL 90 DAY)) AS back_within_90_days,
       ROUND(SUM(EXISTS (SELECT 1
                         FROM orders_only o
                         WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id
                           AND o.order_date  > c.first_order_date
                           AND o.order_date  < c.first_order_date + INTERVAL 90 DAY)) * 100
             / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct
FROM customer_summary c
WHERE c.first_order_date < '2024-10-02';
Result
+-----------+---------------------+------+
| customers | back_within_90_days | pct  |
+-----------+---------------------+------+
|      1830 |                 537 | 29.3 |
+-----------+---------------------+------+

EXISTS (Day 8) asks a yes-or-no question for each customer: "is there an order after the first one, but within 90 days?" It gives 1 or 0, so SUM counts the yeses. The answer, 29.3%, is lower than 38.7%, and that's fine, because it measures something different and more useful: "what share of new customers come back within 90 days?" Every customer was given the same chance, so you can compare this number across months, channels or years, and it means the same thing each time.

3Cohorts: do they keep coming back?

A school doesn't judge its teaching by mixing every pupil from every year into one big average. It follows each batch, the "class of 2023", from the year they joined. That's a cohort: a group of people who started at the same time, followed over time.

For customers, the usual cohort is when they first bought. Then you ask: of the customers who first bought in a quarter, what share bought again one quarter later? Two quarters later? That share is called retention. Here's the whole pattern. It's the longest query so far, so the explanation follows it, step by step:

SQL
WITH orders_only AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, order_id, order_date
    FROM clean_sales
),
activity AS (
    SELECT o.customer_id,
           CONCAT(YEAR(c.first_order_date), '-Q', QUARTER(c.first_order_date)) AS cohort,
           YEAR(c.first_order_date) * 4 + QUARTER(c.first_order_date)          AS cohort_index,
           (YEAR(o.order_date) * 4 + QUARTER(o.order_date))
             - (YEAR(c.first_order_date) * 4 + QUARTER(c.first_order_date))    AS quarters_later
    FROM orders_only o
    JOIN customer_summary c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
),
cohorts AS (
    SELECT cohort,
           cohort_index,
           COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)                                      AS customers,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN quarters_later = 1 THEN customer_id END) AS back_q1,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN quarters_later = 2 THEN customer_id END) AS back_q2,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN quarters_later = 3 THEN customer_id END) AS back_q3,
           COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN quarters_later = 4 THEN customer_id END) AS back_q4
    FROM activity
    GROUP BY cohort, cohort_index
)
SELECT cohort,
       customers,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 1 <= 2024 * 4 + 4 THEN ROUND(back_q1 * 100 / customers) END AS q_plus_1,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 2 <= 2024 * 4 + 4 THEN ROUND(back_q2 * 100 / customers) END AS q_plus_2,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 3 <= 2024 * 4 + 4 THEN ROUND(back_q3 * 100 / customers) END AS q_plus_3,
       CASE WHEN cohort_index + 4 <= 2024 * 4 + 4 THEN ROUND(back_q4 * 100 / customers) END AS q_plus_4
FROM cohorts
ORDER BY cohort_index;
Result
+---------+-----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+
| cohort  | customers | q_plus_1 | q_plus_2 | q_plus_3 | q_plus_4 |
+---------+-----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+
| 2023-Q1 |       243 |       35 |       34 |       33 |       23 |
| 2023-Q2 |       254 |       26 |       26 |       20 |       12 |
| 2023-Q3 |       279 |       34 |       22 |       16 |       15 |
| 2023-Q4 |       313 |       27 |       22 |       18 |       19 |
| 2024-Q1 |       167 |       22 |       19 |       19 |     NULL |
| 2024-Q2 |       274 |       25 |       24 |     NULL |     NULL |
| 2024-Q3 |       299 |       29 |     NULL |     NULL |     NULL |
| 2024-Q4 |       286 |     NULL |     NULL |     NULL |     NULL |
+---------+-----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+

Step by step:

  1. orders_only: one row per order, as in section 2.
  2. activity: for every order, which cohort the customer belongs to, and how many quarters after their first quarter this order came. The trick is YEAR * 4 + QUARTER, which numbers the quarters in one long line: 2023-Q4 is 8096 and 2024-Q1 is 8097. Now "quarters later" is a simple subtraction, even across New Year.
  3. cohorts: Day 8's CASE inside an aggregate, used to pivot. COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN quarters_later = 1 THEN customer_id END) counts the customers who ordered one quarter later. DISTINCT matters: a customer with three orders in that quarter still counts once.
  4. The final SELECT turns counts into percentages, and shows NULL for quarters that haven't happened yet.

That last step is the trap from section 1 in cohort form. Customers who first bought in 2024-Q4 can't have come back "one quarter later", because that quarter is in 2025. Without the CASE, that cell would say 0%, which reads as "nobody came back". The truth is "we don't know yet". 2024 * 4 + 4 is the index of our last quarter, so any cell beyond it gets NULL. That's why the table has a triangle shape: every cohort row is shorter than the one above it.

How to read a cohort table

  • Read across a row to follow one cohort as it ages. Of the 243 customers who first bought in 2023-Q1, 35% bought again in the next quarter, and 23% were still buying a full year later. That was the best cohort.
  • Read down a column to compare cohorts at the same age. One quarter later, between 22% and 35% come back. If that number fell steadily for newer cohorts, it would be a warning sign that the shop is getting worse at keeping people. Here it moves around, without a clear trend.
  • Look at the diagonals for calendar effects. Every cell on the same diagonal is the same calendar quarter, so a good or bad season shows up across several cohorts at once. Compare two neighbours: for the 2023-Q3 cohort, "one quarter later" was the festive 2023-Q4, and 34% came back. For the 2023-Q4 cohort, "one quarter later" was the quiet 2024-Q1, and only 27% did.
  • Mind the sizes. 2024-Q1 had only 167 new customers. With small cohorts, a few people change the percentage a lot, so don't read too much into one cell.

Quarters keep this first table small enough to read. Bigger businesses usually use monthly cohorts, and on Day 22 you'll build those, and measure churn too.

4The website funnel: where do visitors give up?

Picture a shop. 100 people walk in. 65 pick something up to look at it. 18 put something in their basket. 10 walk to the till, and 6 pay. Each step loses people, and the picture of those shrinking numbers looks like a kitchen funnel. Day 10 first saw this shape in web_events. Now let's measure it properly.

The funnel is about sessions (visits), but clean_web_events has one row per event. Grain again. So first, squash each session into one row, with a 1 or 0 for each step it reached. event_type = 'add_to_cart' is 1 or 0 for each event, so MAX over the session's events says whether it happened at least once. Before trusting the funnel, check one thing: does anyone skip a step?

SQL
WITH sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id,
           MAX(event_type = 'product_view')   AS viewed_product,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart')    AS added_to_cart,
           MAX(event_type = 'checkout_start') AS started_checkout,
           MAX(event_type = 'purchase')       AS purchased
    FROM clean_web_events
    GROUP BY session_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*)                                  AS sessions,
       SUM(added_to_cart    AND NOT viewed_product)   AS cart_without_view,
       SUM(started_checkout AND NOT added_to_cart)    AS checkout_without_cart,
       SUM(purchased        AND NOT started_checkout) AS purchase_without_checkout
FROM sessions;
Result
+----------+-------------------+-----------------------+---------------------------+
| sessions | cart_without_view | checkout_without_cart | purchase_without_checkout |
+----------+-------------------+-----------------------+---------------------------+
|    20905 |                 0 |                     0 |                         0 |
+----------+-------------------+-----------------------+---------------------------+

Nobody skips a step, so counting the sessions that reached each step gives a true funnel. (In real tracking data, skips happen: a visitor clicks "buy now" straight from an ad, or the tracking misses an event. If they do, you have to decide whether a session only counts at a step if it reached all the earlier ones. Always check first.)

Now the funnel itself. One more thing from the issues log: on Day 12 we found the tracking was down on the morning of 19 November (issue #13). Sessions that started that day may be missing events, so we leave them out, with HAVING on each session's first event:

SQL
WITH sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id,
           MAX(event_type = 'product_view')   AS viewed_product,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart')    AS added_to_cart,
           MAX(event_type = 'checkout_start') AS started_checkout,
           MAX(event_type = 'purchase')       AS purchased
    FROM clean_web_events
    GROUP BY session_id
    HAVING NOT (MIN(event_time) >= '2024-11-19' AND MIN(event_time) < '2024-11-20')
),
steps AS (
    SELECT 1 AS step, 'Visited the site' AS step_name, COUNT(*) AS sessions FROM sessions
    UNION ALL SELECT 2, 'Viewed a product', SUM(viewed_product)   FROM sessions
    UNION ALL SELECT 3, 'Added to cart',    SUM(added_to_cart)    FROM sessions
    UNION ALL SELECT 4, 'Started checkout', SUM(started_checkout) FROM sessions
    UNION ALL SELECT 5, 'Purchased',        SUM(purchased)        FROM sessions
)
SELECT step,
       step_name,
       sessions,
       ROUND(sessions * 100 / LAG(sessions) OVER (ORDER BY step), 1)         AS pct_of_previous_step,
       ROUND(sessions * 100 / FIRST_VALUE(sessions) OVER (ORDER BY step), 1) AS pct_of_visits
FROM steps
ORDER BY step;
Result
+------+------------------+----------+----------------------+---------------+
| step | step_name        | sessions | pct_of_previous_step | pct_of_visits |
+------+------------------+----------+----------------------+---------------+
|    1 | Visited the site |    20830 |                 NULL |         100.0 |
|    2 | Viewed a product |    13556 |                 65.1 |          65.1 |
|    3 | Added to cart    |     3700 |                 27.3 |          17.8 |
|    4 | Started checkout |     2142 |                 57.9 |          10.3 |
|    5 | Purchased        |     1252 |                 58.5 |           6.0 |
+------+------------------+----------+----------------------+---------------+

The steps CTE stacks five one-row answers on top of each other with UNION ALL, so that window functions can compare them: LAG gives the step before, and FIRST_VALUE gives step 1 (both Day 9). 75 sessions from 19 November were left out, a tiny share, but now the funnel can't be bent by the outage.

Two leaks stand out:

  • Product page to cart: only 27%. Nearly three out of four visitors who look at a product don't add it to their cart. That's the biggest drop, and it usually points at the product page: price, photos, reviews, delivery time.
  • Checkout to purchase: 58.5%. More than 4 in 10 people who start checking out don't finish. They wanted to buy, and something stopped them: a surprise shipping fee, a failed payment, a long form. This is called checkout abandonment, and it's often the cheapest leak to fix. (Day 11's Try this found that NetBanking and card payments fail more often than other methods. That might be part of the story.)

Overall, 6.0% of visits end in a purchase. That's the website's conversion rate.

Same funnel, by device

A single funnel can hide very different stories. Split it by device, showing the rate at each step:

SQL
WITH sessions AS (
    SELECT session_id,
           MAX(device)                        AS device,
           MAX(event_type = 'product_view')   AS viewed_product,
           MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart')    AS added_to_cart,
           MAX(event_type = 'checkout_start') AS started_checkout,
           MAX(event_type = 'purchase')       AS purchased
    FROM clean_web_events
    GROUP BY session_id
    HAVING NOT (MIN(event_time) >= '2024-11-19' AND MIN(event_time) < '2024-11-20')
)
SELECT device,
       COUNT(*)                                                AS sessions,
       ROUND(SUM(viewed_product)   * 100 / COUNT(*), 1)            AS view_pct,
       ROUND(SUM(added_to_cart)    * 100 / SUM(viewed_product), 1) AS cart_pct,
       ROUND(SUM(started_checkout) * 100 / SUM(added_to_cart), 1)  AS checkout_pct,
       ROUND(SUM(purchased)        * 100 / SUM(started_checkout), 1) AS purchase_pct,
       ROUND(SUM(purchased)        * 100 / COUNT(*), 1)            AS conversion_pct
FROM sessions
GROUP BY device
ORDER BY conversion_pct DESC;
Result
+---------+----------+----------+----------+--------------+--------------+----------------+
| device  | sessions | view_pct | cart_pct | checkout_pct | purchase_pct | conversion_pct |
+---------+----------+----------+----------+--------------+--------------+----------------+
| desktop |     4865 |     72.9 |     35.4 |         64.3 |         50.6 |            8.4 |
| mobile  |    14933 |     62.6 |     24.5 |         55.2 |         64.5 |            5.5 |
| tablet  |     1032 |     64.3 |     23.8 |         46.2 |         41.1 |            2.9 |
+---------+----------+----------+----------+--------------+--------------+----------------+

(Each session uses one device, so MAX(device) just picks it up. Each percentage is "of the step before", except conversion_pct, which is of all visits.)

  • Desktop converts best overall, but loses half of its checkouts. Something in the desktop checkout is worth a look.
  • Mobile brings almost three-quarters of all visits, and once mobile shoppers start checking out, they finish more often than anyone. Their problem is earlier: only about 1 in 4 mobile product views becomes an add to cart. A better mobile product page would pay off more than anything else on this list, simply because there are so many mobile visitors.
  • Tablet is weak at every step, but it's only about 5% of visits.

This is the real value of a funnel: not the 6% at the bottom, but knowing which step to fix, for which visitors.

5RFM: who are the best customers, and who are we losing?

Imagine a gym deciding who to call. Someone who came yesterday doesn't need a call. Someone who came three times a week for a year and then stopped two months ago definitely does. Someone who signed up two years ago, came once, and never returned is probably gone. To tell these people apart, you need three facts:

  • R, Recency: how many days since their last order? Fewer is better.
  • F, Frequency: how many orders have they placed?
  • M, Monetary: how much have they spent in total?

Day 8's segments looked only at spend, and Day 12 calculated recency. RFM puts the three together. All three facts are already in customer_summary. The usual next step is to turn each one into a score from 1 to 5, where 5 is best, so they can be combined. Day 9 promised that NTILE(5) would do this: it splits the customers into five equal-sized groups. It works well for recency and spend. But watch what happens with frequency:

SQL
SELECT orders,
       f_score,
       COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM (
    SELECT orders,
           NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY orders, customer_id) AS f_score
    FROM customer_summary
) AS scored
WHERE orders <= 2
GROUP BY orders, f_score
ORDER BY orders, f_score;
Result
+--------+---------+-----------+
| orders | f_score | customers |
+--------+---------+-----------+
|      1 |       1 |       423 |
|      1 |       2 |       423 |
|      1 |       3 |       423 |
|      1 |       4 |        27 |
|      2 |       4 |       202 |
+--------+---------+-----------+

Customers with exactly one order have been given scores of 1, 2, 3 and 4. NTILE must make five equal groups of 423, and 1,296 customers share the value 1, so it has to split identical customers across different groups. A customer with one order could be scored 4 while another with one order is scored 1, just because of their ID. That's not fair, and it's not useful.

The fix is to use NTILE only where values are spread out (days since the last order, money spent), and fixed business rules where many customers share a value (number of orders). Here's the full RFM view:

Build 2: customer_rfm
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW customer_rfm AS
WITH base AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           DATEDIFF('2024-12-31', last_order_date) AS recency_days,   -- our "today"
           orders                                  AS frequency,
           revenue                                 AS monetary
    FROM customer_summary
),
scored AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           recency_days,
           frequency,
           monetary,
           NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY recency_days DESC, customer_id) AS r_score,
           CASE
               WHEN frequency = 1  THEN 1
               WHEN frequency = 2  THEN 2
               WHEN frequency = 3  THEN 3
               WHEN frequency <= 5 THEN 4
               ELSE 5
           END                                                    AS f_score,
           NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY monetary, customer_id)          AS m_score
    FROM base
)
SELECT scored.*,
       CASE
           WHEN r_score >= 4 AND f_score >= 4 THEN 'Champions'
           WHEN r_score >= 3 AND f_score >= 3 THEN 'Loyal'
           WHEN r_score >= 4 AND f_score = 1  THEN 'New'
           WHEN r_score >= 3                  THEN 'Promising'
           WHEN f_score >= 3                  THEN 'At risk'
           ELSE 'Lost'
       END AS segment
FROM scored;

A few things to notice:

  • Recency is sorted DESC, so the customers with the most days since their last order get score 1, and the most recent get 5. For recency, small is good.
  • customer_id is a tie-breaker (Day 9). Without it, customers with the same recency could get different scores each time the query runs.
  • The segment CASE is checked from top to bottom, and the first match wins (Day 8). "Champions" must come before "Loyal", or champions would be labelled loyal. The last line catches everyone left: not recent, and few orders.
  • The rules are a choice. There's no single official set of RFM segments. Different companies use different names and cut-offs. These are written down in the view, where anyone can see them and change them (Day 11's rule).

Let's check three customers you've met before, from Day 11's duplicate accounts:

SQL
SELECT customer_id, recency_days, frequency, ROUND(monetary) AS monetary,
       r_score, f_score, m_score, segment
FROM customer_rfm
WHERE customer_id IN (1, 1247, 2688)
ORDER BY customer_id;

6Customer value: what is a customer worth?

Customer lifetime value (CLV, or LTV) is the money a customer brings in over their whole relationship with the shop. It answers a very practical question: how much can we afford to spend to win a new customer? If a customer is worth ₹2,000 in profit, paying ₹3,000 in ads to win them loses money.

The simplest version is an average:

SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                                AS customers,
       ROUND(SUM(revenue) / SUM(orders))       AS avg_order_value,
       ROUND(SUM(orders) / COUNT(*), 2)        AS orders_per_customer,
       ROUND(SUM(revenue) / COUNT(*))          AS revenue_per_customer,
       ROUND(SUM(gross_profit) / COUNT(*))     AS profit_per_customer
FROM customer_summary;
Result
+-----------+-----------------+---------------------+----------------------+---------------------+
| customers | avg_order_value | orders_per_customer | revenue_per_customer | profit_per_customer |
+-----------+-----------------+---------------------+----------------------+---------------------+
|      2115 |            2327 |                2.68 |                 6228 |                2558 |
+-----------+-----------------+---------------------+----------------------+---------------------+

An average order of ₹2,327, about 2.7 orders per customer, so about ₹6,228 of revenue per customer: that's the classic formula, average order value × orders per customer. In profit, ₹2,558.

But here's the section 1 trap again. Split those customers by the year they first bought:

SQL
SELECT YEAR(first_order_date)     AS first_bought_in,
       COUNT(*)                   AS customers,
       ROUND(AVG(orders), 2)      AS avg_orders,
       ROUND(AVG(revenue))        AS avg_revenue
FROM customer_summary
GROUP BY first_bought_in;
Result
+-----------------+-----------+------------+-------------+
| first_bought_in | customers | avg_orders | avg_revenue |
+-----------------+-----------+------------+-------------+
|            2023 |      1089 |       3.46 |        7918 |
|            2024 |      1026 |       1.85 |        4435 |
+-----------------+-----------+------------+-------------+

Customers from 2023 look almost twice as valuable as customers from 2024. Are the new customers worse? Not necessarily: the 2023 customers have simply had a year longer to keep buying. A "lifetime" average that mixes old and new customers isn't a lifetime value at all.

A fair version: the first 12 months

Give everyone the same window: the value of each customer in the 12 months after their first order. Only customers who first bought in 2023 have had a full 12 months in our data, so we use them. And since the real question is "how much can we spend to win a customer?", let's split by how they found the shop, and use profit, not revenue:

SQL
WITH first_year_customers AS (
    SELECT customer_id, first_order_date
    FROM customer_summary
    WHERE first_order_date < '2024-01-01'
),
first_12_months AS (
    SELECT f.customer_id,
           COUNT(DISTINCT s.order_id)        AS orders,
           SUM(s.line_total)                 AS revenue,
           SUM(s.line_total - s.line_cost)   AS gross_profit
    FROM first_year_customers f
    JOIN clean_sales s
      ON s.customer_id = f.customer_id
     AND s.order_date  < f.first_order_date + INTERVAL 12 MONTH
    GROUP BY f.customer_id
)
SELECT COALESCE(c.acquisition_channel, 'Not recorded') AS found_us_through,
       COUNT(*)                     AS customers,
       ROUND(AVG(m.orders), 1)      AS orders_12m,
       ROUND(AVG(m.revenue))        AS revenue_12m,
       ROUND(AVG(m.gross_profit))   AS profit_12m
FROM first_12_months m
JOIN clean_customers c ON c.customer_id = m.customer_id
GROUP BY found_us_through
UNION ALL
SELECT 'All channels', COUNT(*), ROUND(AVG(orders), 1), ROUND(AVG(revenue)), ROUND(AVG(gross_profit))
FROM first_12_months
ORDER BY profit_12m DESC;
Result
+------------------+-----------+------------+-------------+------------+
| found_us_through | customers | orders_12m | revenue_12m | profit_12m |
+------------------+-----------+------------+-------------+------------+
| Email            |        50 |        3.2 |        7148 |       2921 |
| Organic Search   |       292 |        3.1 |        6897 |       2847 |
| Google Ads       |       204 |        2.9 |        6901 |       2756 |
| Direct           |        89 |        2.9 |        6793 |       2702 |
| All channels     |      1089 |        2.9 |        6629 |       2685 |
| Instagram        |       206 |        2.9 |        6431 |       2622 |
| Referral         |       123 |        3.0 |        6397 |       2552 |
| Facebook         |        93 |        2.6 |        5996 |       2406 |
| Not recorded     |        32 |        2.6 |        5174 |       2075 |
+------------------+-----------+------------+-------------+------------+

How it works: first_year_customers picks the customers who have had 12 full months. The join brings in only their orders from before first order + 12 months, a half-open window that's different for every customer (Day 12). Then we average by channel, and UNION ALL adds an "All channels" row for comparison. (clean_customers has one row per account, and our customer_id is the master ID, so the join picks up the channel from each person's first account.)

So: a new customer is worth about ₹2,685 in gross profit in their first year. Customers from email and organic search are worth a little more, Facebook a little less. If winning a customer through Facebook ads costs more than about ₹2,400, those customers lose money in their first year, before delivery and other costs are even counted. That's a sentence a marketing manager can act on.

Watch the group sizes. Email tops the table, but with only 50 customers. A handful of big spenders could put it there by luck. Organic search has 292, so its number is far more reliable. Before you say "email customers are the most valuable", check how many customers stand behind the number. And notice that the differences between channels are fairly small: the honest summary is "Facebook customers are worth a bit less", not "email is the best channel".

Real CLV models. What we built is historical value: what customers actually spent. Data science teams also predict future value with statistical models, using exactly the facts in customer_summary (recency, frequency, spend, first order date) as inputs. The SQL you wrote today is the first half of that work.

7Telling the story

Five tables of numbers are not an answer. The last step of every analysis is to turn the numbers into a few sentences that someone can act on. Lead with the finding, give the number that proves it, and say what to do. Here's what you could send the owner today:

Customer health check, as of 31 December 2024

  • Most customers buy once. 61% of customers have placed only one order. Those who do return come back fast: 29% of new customers order again within 90 days, and almost half of all repeat orders come within a month of the previous one. Action: focus follow-up on the first 30 to 90 days after a first order.
  • Retention is steady, not falling. Each quarter, between 22% and 35% of the previous quarter's new customers buy again, with no downward trend across cohorts.
  • A small group carries the business. 295 Champions (14% of buyers) bring in 43% of revenue. Action: protect them.
  • 136 valuable customers are slipping away. They used to order about 5 times and spend about ₹11,000 each, but have been quiet for over a year on average. Action: a win-back campaign, starting with the biggest spenders.
  • The website loses most people between the product page and the cart, especially on mobile, which brings almost three-quarters of all visits. And 4 in 10 checkouts are abandoned. Action: improve the mobile product page, and find out why checkouts fail.
  • A new customer is worth about ₹2,700 in gross profit in their first year (₹2,400 if they came from Facebook). Action: keep the cost of winning a customer well below this.

Every sentence points back to a query you wrote, so if anyone asks "how do you know?", you can show them. That's what makes an analyst trusted.

🎯 Interview corner

Product and data analyst interviews love these patterns, because they show you can turn data into business answers. Expect to write one of them and explain the trade-offs.

Concept questions

Q1What is cohort analysis, and why not just look at monthly totals?

A cohort is a group of users who started at the same time, usually by first purchase or sign-up month. Cohort analysis follows each group over time, for example the share still buying 1, 2, 3 months later. Monthly totals mix old and new customers, so strong customer acquisition can hide poor retention (or the other way round). Cohorts separate "are we winning new customers?" from "do customers stay?"

Q2What's the difference between repeat rate and retention rate?

Repeat rate is the share of customers who have ever bought more than once: one number for the whole customer base. Retention rate is time-based: of the customers in a cohort, the share active in a later period (for example, one quarter after their first). Retention shows how loyalty changes over time and across cohorts. Repeat rate doesn't.

Q3Why do recent customers or cohorts often look worse, and how do you fix it?

They've had less time to come back, so their repeat rates, retention cells and lifetime values are cut off early. Fixes: use a fixed window (for example, repeat within 90 days, or value in the first 12 months) and include only customers who have had that full window; show "not yet known" cells as NULL rather than 0.

Q4How do you build a conversion funnel in SQL?

Choose the unit (sessions or users) and the steps. Collapse events to one row per unit with a flag per step, such as MAX(event_type = 'add_to_cart') or MAX(CASE WHEN ... THEN 1 ELSE 0 END). Sum the flags per step, then compute each step as a percentage of the previous step and of the first. Check whether units skip steps, and decide whether a step only counts if the earlier steps happened. Break it down by device or source to find where to act.

Q5What is RFM segmentation?

Scoring each customer on Recency (days since last purchase), Frequency (number of purchases) and Monetary value (total spend), usually 1 to 5 each, then grouping score combinations into segments such as Champions, At risk or Lost. It's simple, explainable, needs only order data, and leads straight to marketing actions.

Q6Why can NTILE give misleading RFM scores?

NTILE forces equal-sized groups. When many customers share a value, which is very common for frequency (most customers order once), identical customers get split across different scores, depending only on the sort order. Use fixed thresholds for heavily tied measures, and always add a tie-breaker to ORDER BY so the scores are repeatable.

Q7How would you estimate customer lifetime value with SQL?

Simple version: average order value × orders per customer (or total revenue / customers), ideally using gross profit rather than revenue. Better: fix the window, such as value in the first 12 months, for customers who have had 12 months, and compare it across cohorts or acquisition channels. Compare CLV with the cost of acquiring a customer. Predictive CLV needs statistical models, but they start from the same customer-level table.

Q8Should a funnel count sessions or users?

It depends on the question. Session funnels measure how well a single visit converts, and work even for visitors who aren't logged in. User funnels measure whether people eventually buy, even across several visits, but need a reliable user ID. In our data only about a quarter of sessions have a customer_id, so a session funnel is the right choice.

Quick recap

  • Most business questions are about customers or sessions, so first build a table at that grain (customer_summary), and check it adds up to a number you trust.
  • Repeat purchase: share with 2+ orders, and the gaps between orders with LAG. 61% buy once, and those who return mostly do so within 30 to 60 days.
  • Give everyone the same time. Recent customers haven't had time to come back. Use fixed windows (90 days, 12 months), include only customers who've had the full window, and show "not yet known" as NULL, not 0.
  • Cohorts: group customers by when they first bought, pivot with COUNT(DISTINCT CASE ...), and read rows (ageing), columns (comparing cohorts) and diagonals (calendar effects).
  • Funnels: roll events up to sessions with MAX(event_type = ...), check for skipped steps, stack the steps with UNION ALL, and compare with LAG and FIRST_VALUE. Split by device to find which step to fix.
  • RFM: recency, frequency, monetary, scored 1 to 5. NTILE for spread-out values, fixed rules where values tie, then a CASE for segments.
  • CLV: average order value × orders per customer, or better, profit in a fixed first-year window, by channel. Check how many customers stand behind each number.
  • Finish with the story: finding, number, action.
